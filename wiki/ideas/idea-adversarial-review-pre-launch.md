# Idea: The Adversarial Review — Add a Pre-Launch Hostile Reading Pass

## The Idea
Before any campaign, ad, or major piece of content goes live, run one structured review pass by a person whose single job in that session is to find everything that could go wrong — the worst reading, the misinterpretation, the cultural implication, the meme that could be made from it. This is not the creative review, the brand consistency review, or the legal review. It is a distinct function: adversarial simulation.

## The Problem It Solves

The marketing failures in [[Worst Marketing Patterns]] share a structural root cause: the gap between internal perception and external reception. Campaigns that caused reputational damage — American Eagle's racial implication, Gatorade's meme co-option, celebrity fit mismatches — all passed through internal review processes. The problem is not that people weren't reviewing. The problem is that the review was conducted by people who already liked the idea, who had been through the creative process, and who were not positioned to find the worst reading.

Internal review optimizes for approval, not for adversarial discovery.

## How to Run the Adversarial Review

**Who runs it:** Someone outside the creative team — ideally someone with no history with the campaign — whose role is explicitly to play the hostile audience. In larger organizations this can be a rotating function. In smaller ones, it can be an external person brought in specifically for this step.

**The four questions to ask:**

1. **The worst headline test:** If a journalist who dislikes this brand wrote a piece about this campaign, what would the headline say? Write it. If the headline is plausible and damaging, the campaign has a problem.

2. **The meme test:** What is the most uncomfortable meme that could be made from the central image, phrase, or concept? If the answer is obvious to the adversarial reviewer, it will be obvious to the internet within hours of launch.

3. **The identity subtraction test:** Remove the brand name from all materials. Does the campaign's core message, imagery, or association now imply something the brand would not want attributed to it? (The American Eagle test: with the brand name removed, what does the combination of model, phrase, and visual composition suggest?)

4. **The fit test for celebrity/creator campaigns:** Has this person ever publicly said or done something that directly contradicts the brand's product promise? Search "[name] + [product category] + quotes." If Zendaya has said she's not a runner, this takes two minutes to find.

**What to do with findings:**

- **Fixable before launch:** Change the element. The adversarial review's value is finding problems when they are still cheap to fix.
- **Unfixable but survivable:** Document the risk, prepare a response statement, and decide whether to proceed with eyes open.
- **Campaign-level risk:** Kill or substantially rework the concept. The cost of a bad campaign is multiples of whatever the pivot costs.

## When to Run It

- Any campaign with a celebrity or high-profile creator
- Any campaign that uses a cultural moment, news hook, or social issue as a frame
- Any product imagery that features people (check for implicit associations the creative team may not have noticed)
- Any campaign using a phrase, hashtag, or verbal hook that could be repurposed against the brand
- Any AI-generated content that will be presented as photography or videography (check for anatomical errors, physical impossibilities, backwards elements)

## The Minimum Version for Small Teams

If there is no budget or structure for a formal adversarial review, run this 15-minute exercise before posting anything campaign-sized:

1. Show the creative to one person who was not involved in its production and has not seen it before
2. Ask them: "Tell me the worst thing someone could say about this"
3. Show it to one person who belongs to the demographic most likely to be offended if the campaign has an implicit issue
4. Ask the same question
5. If either person produces an answer that surprises you, you have found something worth addressing
