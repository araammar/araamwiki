# Concept: The Size Of The Pie Fallacy
tags: [leads, Hormozi, scaling, mental-model, fallacy, market-saturation]

## Summary / Definition

The mental error that keeps most businesses small: **assuming the tiny slice of the universe you're advertising to is the entire available market.**

> *"A small business uses one of the core four, on one platform, in one specific way, with a very targeted audience. And in that same space, advertising the same way, there may only be a handful of other competitors. They mistakenly assume the tiny slice of the universe they advertise to is the entire available market! This is why most businesses stay small."*

— Alex Hormozi, *$100M Leads*

---

## The Mechanism

Most business owners look at:
- One Core Four activity (e.g., paid ads on Facebook)
- One platform (e.g., Facebook)
- One specific way (e.g., one ad format, one targeting style)
- One audience (e.g., chiropractors in 3 zip codes)

In that *intersection*, there are usually 3-5 visible competitors. The business owner extrapolates: *"There are 3-5 of us competing for this market. The market must be tapped."*

But the intersection is one tiny sub-slice. The full market is vastly larger. They've confused **their visible competitive landscape** with **the actual addressable market**.

---

## The Chiropractor Story

The story that opens [[more-better-new-framework]]:

A business owner claimed he'd saturated chiropractors at $2M/yr revenue, $30K/mo on Facebook ads only.

Hormozi's diagnostic questions:
- Other platforms? **None.**
- Content for chiropractors? **None.**
- Cold outreach? **None.**

The actual data point Hormozi countered with:

> *"And the $30k you spend, on one platform, for a two million dollar business, saturated the $15.1B chiropractor industry?"*

That's $30K out of $15.1 *billion*. Even at maximum theoretical ad efficiency, this business is touching **less than 0.0002%** of its addressable market.

A second chiropractor in the audience immediately volunteered: *"I'm in the chiropractor niche too, and I spent $30k in advertising, across four platforms, last week."*

That competitor was spending **4× the budget across 4× the platforms** in the same week. **Same niche. 16× the total advertising presence. Both still scaling.**

---

## The Weight Loss Example

Hormozi cites another conversation:

> *"A different entrepreneur making about $3,000,000 per year in the weight loss space . . . worried increasing his ad spend past $40,000 per month would saturate his ad platform. For context, that platform has over 1B active daily users. And he was selling weight loss . . . in America . . . a $60B industry."*

$40K/mo on a 1-billion-user platform selling into a $60B market. The "saturation" worry is fantasy.

> *"Silly."*

---

## Why The Fallacy Persists

> *"For many, saying 'I'm as big as I can get' is much easier than saying 'I'm not as good at advertising as I thought.' This false argument keeps entrepreneurs everywhere poorer than they should be."*

The fallacy is comforting:
- Removes blame from the owner (the market is the constraint)
- Removes the need for skill acquisition (no point if maxed)
- Removes the need for risk (don't try new things)

Confronting the fallacy means admitting:
- Your advertising could be better
- Your visibility is smaller than you thought
- You have to learn new things to grow

Most owners would rather believe the fallacy.

---

## The Tells

If you find yourself thinking any of these, suspect the fallacy:

- "We've saturated the [niche] market"
- "There's no more demand"
- "All the [target customers] already know about us"
- "Adding more ad spend wouldn't change anything"
- "The platform has run out of audience"

Then check:
- How many Core Four activities are you running? (Usually 1)
- How many platforms? (Usually 1)
- How many ad placements within that platform? (Usually 1-2)
- What % of your TAM are you actually reaching? (Usually <0.1%)

The arithmetic almost always reveals the fallacy.

---

## The Solution

Apply [[more-better-new-framework|More-Better-New]] in order:

1. **More** on the current activity (Rule of 100)
2. **Better** at the constraint (testing)
3. **New** placements within the current platform
4. **New** platforms running the same method
5. **New** Core Four activities entirely

There are 4 Core Four activities × dozens of platforms × multiple placements per platform = **hundreds of unique advertising slots** for any niche.

A business saturating one slot is using **<1%** of its true advertising capacity.

---

## The Wider Implication

> *"There are more leads out there than you can possibly imagine."*

The Size of the Pie Fallacy is the single most common reason businesses plateau at $1M-$5M/year. Once corrected, the path to $10M+ is mechanical:
- Add more activities
- Across more platforms
- With more lead-getters (see Lead Getters section)
- Until your operational constraint, not your advertising constraint, becomes the bottleneck

## The Acquisition.com Operating Logic

> *"I've used a framework to unlock those leads over and over again and now you can use it too."*

Hormozi explicitly cites this as a pillar of his portfolio company growth process. When a company plateaus, the first diagnostic is the Size of the Pie audit:
- What's your visible competitive landscape?
- What's your true addressable market?
- What's the ratio?

If the ratio is even 1:100 (i.e., you're hitting 1% of TAM), there's at least 100× growth available before saturation becomes a real concern.

---

## Application For Caret/Colandrum

Before declaring any niche "tapped":

1. **List every Core Four activity** you're currently doing
2. **List every platform** you're on
3. **List every placement** you're running within those platforms
4. **Estimate TAM** for your defined avatar
5. **Calculate % reach** you actually have

If reach is < 5% of TAM, the fallacy is in play. Apply More-Better-New.

For agencies as Colandrum's avatar — there are tens of thousands of US agencies alone. Touching 1,000 of them via cold outreach is < 5% of the addressable market. The pie is still nearly whole.
