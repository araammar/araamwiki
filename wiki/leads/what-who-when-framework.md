# Framework: The What-Who-When Framework
tags: [leads, Hormozi, paid-ads, value-equation, framework, copywriting]

## Summary / Definition

Hormozi's framework for the **value element** of paid ads (the middle of [[paid-ads-callout-value-cta|Callout + Value + CTA]]). The framework lets you generate an arbitrary number of ad angles from a single offer by varying three lenses:

- **What** — the 8 key value elements (4 drivers + 4 opposites)
- **Who** — the people around the prospect who affect their status
- **When** — past, present, future timeline

The output: a list of distinct ad angles, each compelling, each touching a different psychological lever.

> *"The customer isn't a moron. She's your wife."*
>
> — David Ogilvy (quoted by Hormozi)

---

## The Underlying Logic

> *"A good advertisement, paid or not, uses clear and simple ways to answer the question: why should I be interested in your thing?"*

Most ads answer this with the same 1-2 angles. The What-Who-When framework forces you to find **many** angles, then pick the best for each ad creative.

This is the framework's promise: take your single offer and generate 20+ distinct ads from it without lying or exaggerating.

---

## The What — 8 Key Elements (4 Drivers + 4 Opposites)

The full [[value-equation]] from $100M Offers, applied to ad copy. Each driver has an opposite that's equally compelling.

### Driver 1: Dream Outcome ↔ Nightmare

**Dream Outcome (carrot)**: Show the maximum benefit they can achieve.

**Nightmare (stick)**: Show the worst hassles/pain of going without your solution.

A good ad uses both. The dream pulls; the nightmare pushes.

### Driver 2: Perceived Likelihood ↔ Risk

**Likelihood (carrot)**: Minimize past failures, emphasize success of similar people, give assurances (authority, guarantees), show higher chance than current method.

**Risk (stick)**: Show how risky it is to *not* act. What will life be like if they keep doing what they've always done? Past failures repeating, problems compounding.

### Driver 3: Time Delay ↔ Speed

**Speed (carrot)**: How much faster they get what they want with your solution.

**Time Delay (stick)**: How slow their current trajectory is. Show they'll **never** get what they want at the current rate.

### Driver 4: Effort & Sacrifice ↔ Ease

**Ease (carrot)**: How you avoid the bad stuff and keep the good stuff while still getting the outcome.

**Effort & Sacrifice (stick)**: How much work and skill it'll take without you. The bad stuff they keep enduring. The good stuff they keep giving up. Worst of all: how they're already working hard and getting nowhere.

## The Who — Multiple Perspectives

> *"Humans are primarily status driven. And the status of one human comes from how the other humans treat them. So if your product or service changes how other people treat your customer, which it does in some way, it pays to show how."*

Two groups to outline:
1. **The status-gainer** — your customer
2. **The status-givers** — the people around them: Spouse, Kids, Parents, Extended Family, Colleagues, Bosses, Friends, Rivals, Competitors

Each perspective gives different angles on the same outcome.

### Worked Example: Weight Loss

If your offer is risk-free, you can frame it through different Whos:
- **Spouse** won't nag about the purchase (no financial risk)
- **Kids** notice you're less stressed/distracted by work
- **Competitors** notice their phones ring less (customers flow to you)
- **Business owner buddies** say "business must be good" when you pull up in a new car at the golf range

> *"These are all added benefits to the prospect we'd miss out on if we only looked at it from their own perspective."*

### The Multiplier Effect

Each Who × each What driver = a new angle.

Example for "your spouse" + 4 drivers:
- Spouse + Dream Outcome → how your spouse perceives the results
- Spouse + Likelihood → spouse seeing the proof, no nagging
- Spouse + Speed → spouse noticing fast change
- Spouse + Ease → spouse not having to deal with your suffering

Repeat for kids, parents, friends, etc. **You produce 30+ angles from one offer.**

## The When — Past, Present, Future

> *"People often only think of how their decisions affect the here and now. But if we want to be extra compelling (and we do), we should also explain what their decisions led to in the past and what their decisions could lead to in the future."*

Show their timeline:

### Weight Loss Through Their Timeline (Pain Frame)
- **Past**: Getting teased as a kid
- **Present**: Struggling to button their favorite jeans
- **Future**: Moving up another belt loop

### Weight Loss Through Their Spouse's Eyes (Their Timeline)
- **Past**: Nightmare moments
- **Present**: Embarrassment
- **Future**: What it'll look like 10 years from now

### Weight Loss Through Their Kids' Eyes
- **Past**: Kid asking why other kids tease them (passed-down habits)
- **Present**: Kids complain about other kids' dads at practice (you don't participate)
- **Future**: Doctor said they might not walk their daughter down the aisle

---

## Putting It All Together — The What-Who-When Sentence

The framework's output combines all three:

> *"How fast (WHAT - Speed) they fit into 'that suit your wife loves that didn't fit but does now' (WHO - spouse perspective) in the future (WHEN)."*

> *"How their kids (WHO) month after month (WHEN) got more interested in eating healthy and tagging along during workouts (WHAT - Effort/Sacrifice)."*

> *"How they (WHO - self) catch a look at themselves in a reflection in the mall in a few months (WHEN) and realize 'stuff actually fits me in this store' (WHAT - Dream Outcome)."*

Each of these is a distinct ad angle. You can rotate them across your ad creative library to test which lands best.

---

## Why This Produces Better Ads

> *"When we combine: everything we can to get the prospect going toward the four value drivers, while also getting them away from their opposites... the many perspectives we can show them gaining status... and different timelines for each... This adds up to why they should be interested. And now we have a lot of ways to get them interested!"*

The framework forces you to:
1. Cover both carrots (drivers) and sticks (opposites)
2. Cover the prospect's own perspective AND social perspectives
3. Cover past failures, present pain, future risk/reward

Most ads cover 1-2 angles. A What-Who-When-disciplined library covers all 12+.

---

## Long Ads Vs Short Ads

> *"The only difference between long ads and short ads is how many angles we have time to cover from the copywriting framework. Longer ads use more. Shorter ads use fewer."*

Add or take away angles based on the platform — **but always keep the callout (first 5 seconds) and CTA (what to do next) the same** regardless of length.

## Application For Caret/Colandrum

Take Colandrum's single offer (e.g., "We help agencies stop losing pitches to bigger competitors") and apply What-Who-When:

### What (8 elements)
- **Dream**: Win more pitches you should already be winning
- **Nightmare**: Watch competitors with deeper data win clients you brought to the table
- **Likelihood**: Live demo on their next pitch, signal-driven proof
- **Risk**: Continue losing $X of pitches per quarter to inferior agencies with better data
- **Speed**: Pitch with intelligence in 24 hours
- **Time Delay**: Cold-pitch agencies dying as data becomes table stakes
- **Ease**: Plug into your existing tools, no workflow change
- **Effort**: Manual research per pitch costs hours

### Who
- **Self**: Their agency wins
- **Partners**: Confidence in their judgment
- **Clients**: "These guys actually understand my business"
- **Junior Account Managers**: Easier to prep for pitches
- **Competitors**: Lose pitches they thought they had locked

### When
- **Past**: Pitches they lost in the last 6 months
- **Present**: The pitch they're prepping right now
- **Future**: Next quarter's pipeline

That's **20+ distinct angles** from one offer. The ad library writes itself.
