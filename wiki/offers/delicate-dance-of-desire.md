# Concept: The Delicate Dance Of Desire
tags: [offers, Hormozi, supply-demand, psychology, scarcity, urgency, Hormozi Law, Naval]

## Summary / Definition

The mental model behind every scarcity, urgency, bonus, and guarantee enhancement: **supply and demand are inversely correlated, and skilled marketers consciously manipulate the ratio between them to maximize demand and price.**

> *"Fundamentally, all marketing exists to influence the supply and demand curve. We artificially increase the demand for our products and services through some sort of persuasive communication. When we increase the demand, we can sell more units. When we decrease supply, we can sell those units for more money. The 'perfect profit combination' is lots of demand, and very little supply, or perceived supply."*

---

## The Naval Quote At The Foundation

> *"Desire is a contract you make with yourself to be unhappy until you get what you want."*
>
> — Naval Ravikant (cited by Hormozi)

If desire is a function of *not having*, then the moment a customer gets what they want, the desire for it disappears. The implication for marketing: **to increase demand, you must decrease or delay satisfaction.**

This is counterintuitive but operationally critical. The natural impulse of an eager business owner is to satisfy demand as fast as possible. The Hormozi-Naval frame says: satisfy slower, sell fewer, and watch demand actually *grow*.

---

## The Two-Scenario Workshop Example

Hormozi gives a concrete illustration. You promote a two-day workshop. You whisper it's coming. Tease benefits. Shout it's launching in a week. Then you launch.

**Scenario 1:** Sell 10 workshops at $500 each = $5,000 (everyone who wanted in got in)

**Scenario 2:** Sell 2 one-day private workshops at $5,000 each = $10,000 (80% of the original pyramid didn't get in)

The two scenarios produce different *outcomes* AND different *future states*.

### Demand Is Fractal (80/20)

> *"I've found demand to be fractal (80/20). In other words, one fifth of the prospects are willing to pay five times the price (or more)."*

So in the example: maybe 10 people would pay $500, but 2 of them would pay $5,000 — same people, different price elasticity. Scenario 2 captures the highest-paying segment of the pyramid.

### The Aftermath Matters More Than The Sale

After Scenario 1: 0 unsatisfied demand. Next launch, you start from zero.

After Scenario 2: 8 unsatisfied prospects + new prospects who heard about it. Demand *builds* between launches.

> *"On top of that, if people see that others who 'were able to get in' are loving it, it would further increase their desire. And the next time, they would act with more urgency, and be willing to pay more for the same thing than they originally did."*

Next round of Scenario 2: open 3 spots at the same price, sell them out instantly, still leaving unsatisfied demand.

If you keep running Scenario 1 (selling out at low price each time), each iteration sells *fewer* because all desire keeps getting satisfied. Eventually you sell zero.

> *"This is the sad state many businesses find themselves in always trying to generate more demand to make another quick sale."*

## Hormozi's Law

> **"The longer you delay the ask, the bigger the ask you can make."**
>
> **"The longer the runway, the bigger the plane that can take off."**

This is the foundational principle behind product launches, waiting lists, pre-orders, and scarcity-driven pricing. The patience to defer satisfaction translates directly into pricing power.

---

## The Operating Mantra

> *"We must endeavor to keep our supply (and satisfaction of desire) under the demand that we are able to generate. This maximizes profits and keeps desire ravenous in our customer base. This is the real key to never going hungry."*

In other words: deliberately under-supply demand. Every time. Forever. The discomfort of leaving sales on the table is more than offset by the compounding desire in your prospect base.

---

## The Significant Other Analogy

> *"If you sleep with your significant other every day they have less desire than if you haven't slept with them for a week. We want the ravenous prospect, not merely the aroused."*

Crude but accurate. Frequency satiates. Restraint amplifies. This is the natural state of desire across every domain — sex, food, attention, status. Marketing operates inside the same psychological reality.

---

## The Symmetric Failure Mode

Hormozi notes the equation has two failure modes:

1. **Satisfy zero demand (zero supply):** You make no money. Eventually leave people feeling rejected (takes longer than you'd think).
2. **Satisfy all demand:** You kill the golden goose. Next month you have to generate fresh demand from scratch.

> *"Mastering supply and demand comes from the elegant dance between the two."*

The right answer is always *somewhere between fully open and fully closed* — and skewed deliberately toward the closed side.

## The Arnold Schwarzenegger Fundraiser (The Dance In Practice)

May 2019. Arnold's home. After School All Stars Fundraiser. The ticket price was raised from $15,000 to $25,000 per ticket. Why?

George (a major donor and luxury watch dealer) gave Ben (CEO of the charity) the advice:

> *"That's right. When demand increases, cut supply."*

They had more demand than ever. So they raised prices and cut ticket count. The night ended up being the **most successful in the charity's history — $5,400,000 from 100 people ($54,000/head average donation)**.

Hormozi's takeaway:

> *"People want what they can't have. People want what other people want. People want things only a select few have access to."*

> *"It was a masterful display of human psychology at work in a setting where people were knowingly over-paying for products. The products remained unchanged, yet within this setting, an item that wouldn't sell at a different venue for $10,000 sold for $100,000."*

## The Authority/Celebrity Scarcity Connection

> *"The reason an authority (like a doctor), a celebrity (like Oprah), or a celebrity authority (like Dr. Oz or Dr. Phil) can charge egregious rates is because of implied demand. People assume that there is a lot of demand for their time, and, therefore, not a big supply of it. As a result, it must be expensive."*

Implied demand creates scarcity even before any explicit scarcity tactic is deployed. The mere *perception* that many people want your time is enough to inflate your pricing power.

This is also why charging premium prices itself becomes a self-fulfilling status signal: high price → implied demand → more demand → higher prices justified.

## The "0 Fucks Given" Negotiating Insight

> *"There is little that substitutes for incredible demand. You can try and fake it, but there is a special type of '0 fucks given' vibe that's hard to replicate when you truly do not need a person's money (or even want it)."*

> *"The person who needs the exchange less always has the upper hand. I always try to remember that. It's one of the negotiating and pricing principles that has best served me in my life."*

Demand on your side of the table = leverage. Demand on theirs = no leverage. Engineer your supply/demand ratio so the demand is theirs, not yours.

## The $50,000-A-Day Anecdote

Hormozi tells the story of being offered $50,000 for a single day of his time by two different people who needed help scaling past $1M/mo. He didn't accept because he was making more than $50,000/day in profit from his business and didn't want the distraction.

> *"After the event concluded and I was speaking with Leila, I realized how I had somehow become 'one of those people I had always wondered about.' It was a very surreal experience for me. I finally understood how premium prices were truly made . . . simple supply and demand."*

This is the dance fully internalized: he had become a market with extraordinary demand and tiny supply, and the premium was a natural consequence of that ratio.

## How To Apply

When designing any launch, promotion, or offer:

1. **Generate demand before announcing supply.** Tease. Whisper. Build anticipation.
2. **Cap supply explicitly.** Use scarcity (numbers) or urgency (time) — or both.
3. **Sell out before satisfying full demand.** Leave unsatisfied prospects.
4. **Publicize the sell-out.** Tell everyone you ran out. The social proof compounds.
5. **Repeat with slightly higher prices or slightly less supply next round.**

The compounding nature is what produces the long-term pricing power. Each iteration raises the bar.
