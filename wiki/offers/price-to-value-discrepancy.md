# Concept: Price-to-Value Discrepancy
tags: [offers, Hormozi, pricing, value, Warren Buffett, psychology]

## Summary / Definition

The mechanical insight that drives every pricing and offer decision in the book:

> *"The reason people buy anything is to get a deal. They believe what they are getting (VALUE) is worth more than what they are giving in exchange for it (PRICE). The moment the value they receive dips below what they are paying, they stop buying from you. This price to value discrepancy is what you need to avoid at all costs."*

— Hormozi, citing Warren Buffett: *"Price is what you pay. Value is what you get."*

---

## The Two Ways To Widen The Discrepancy

There are only two levers:

1. **Lower the price** (simplest, but usually wrong — see below)
2. **Raise the value**

> *"The simplest way to increase the gap between price to value is by lowering the price. It's also, most of the time, the wrong decision for the business."*

Why lowering price is wrong:
- You can only go down to $0 (limited downside)
- But you can go infinitely high in value (unlimited upside)
- Lowering price triggers the death spiral of the [[commoditization-and-pricing-power|virtuous cycle of price]]

Unless you have a revolutionary way to cut costs 10× compared to your competition, don't compete on price.

---

## The Grand Slam Goal

> *"The goal of our Grand Slam Offer will be to get more people to say yes at a higher price by increasing our value to price discrepancy. In other words, we will raise our price only after we have sufficiently increased our value. This way, they still get a great deal (think buying $100,000 of value for $10,000). It's 'money at a discount.'"*

The customer should perceive they're getting a steal. Premium price doesn't mean *feels expensive* — it means *feels like an absurd deal at this price*.

---

## "Money At A Discount" Framing

This is Hormozi's mental shortcut for offer construction:
- If a prospect is paying $10K and getting $100K of value → they buy
- If a prospect is paying $10K and getting $12K of value → they hesitate
- If a prospect is paying $10K and getting $8K of value → they refuse

The math is the *prospect's perception*, not your cost. Your job is to maximize their perceived value, not your spend on delivering it.

---

## The Bonus Mechanic Explained

This concept is also why **bonuses beat discounts** for closing deals:

> *"Whenever trying to close a deal, never discount the main offer. It teaches your customers that your prices are negotiable (which is terrible). Adding bonuses to increase value to close the deal is far superior to cutting prices. It puts you in a position of strength and goodwill rather than weakness."*

When you add a bonus:
- Price stays anchored
- Value goes up
- Discrepancy widens
- Prospect's "feels like a steal" sensation increases

When you discount:
- Price drops
- Value stays the same
- Discrepancy is the same dollar amount but feels less impressive
- Prospect learns your real price is lower → trust erodes

See [[bonus-stacking-system]] for the full mechanic.

## The Anchoring Logic

> *"They establish the price, then they expand upon it until you feel it's such a good deal it would be stupid to pass it up."*

(Hormozi's commentary on classic infomercials with the "but wait, there's more" structure.)

The price is stated **first**. Then bonus after bonus expands the value. Each bonus widens the discrepancy. By the end, the prospect's brain has snapped: *"the value is too high relative to the stated price, I have to buy."*

This is why infomercials sold one knife for $38.95 then threw in 37 other knives, sharpeners, pans, and guarantees. Each addition wasn't just more stuff — it was a wider price-to-value gap. They wouldn't have used this format for decades unless it worked.

> *"The reason this works is we are increasing the prospect's price-to-value discrepancy by increasing the value delivered instead of cutting the price."*

## How To Engineer A Big Discrepancy In Your Offer

The full [[5-step-grand-slam-creation]] process is designed for one thing: maximize this discrepancy.

The arithmetic Hormozi walks through:

**A Gym Launch bundle example:**
- Foolproof Bargain Grocery System: $1,000 value
- Ready in 5min Busy Parent Cooking Guide: $600 value
- Personalized Lick Your Fingers Good Meal Plan: $500 value
- Fat Burning Workouts: $699 value
- Ultimate Tone Up While You Travel Blueprint: $199 value
- "Never Fall Off" Accountability System: $1,000 value
- "Live It Up While Slimming Down" Eating Out System: $349 value
- **Total Value: $4,351**
- **Price: $599**

Discrepancy: ~7.3× value-to-price ratio. Author note: most facilities now sell similar bundles for $2,400–$5,200. The model scales as the operator's confidence and conviction increases.

## The Three Things The Bundle Does

> *"The bundle does three core things:*
> *1. Solves all the perceived problems (not just some)*
> *2. Gives you the conviction that what you're selling is one of a kind (very important)*
> *3. Makes it impossible to compare or confuse your business or offering with the one down the street."*

The third point is the discrepancy in action — a bundle that can't be compared can't be commoditized. Hence: the only relevant comparison for the prospect is *price-to-value*, not *price-to-competitor*.

---

## The Warren Buffett Rule As Operating Principle

> *"Price is what you pay. Value is what you get."*

Internalized as an offer-design principle:
- Your customer is **buying value, not paying price**
- Your job is to maximize *what they get*, not optimize *what they pay*
- Price increases are perceived as fair when value increases more
- Price decreases destroy more value than they save in cost

## The Photography Client Case Study

One of Hormozi's portfolio companies (he has equity in it) was a photography studio. Implementing the book's tactics over two years:

- Average ticket: $300 → **$1,500** (5× increase)
- Time per customer: less
- Customer satisfaction: higher
- Profit per week: $1,000 → **$38,000** (38× increase)
- Scaled to multiple locations
- Donated nearly $500,000 to children's charities

> *"None of that would have been possible without figuring out what people valued most, tripling down on it, and ruthlessly eliminating everything else."*

The 5× price increase didn't reduce demand — it widened the price-to-value discrepancy in the customer's mind. Customers voted with dollars that what the company provides now is *better* than what it did before.

## What This Concept Replaces

This concept replaces a common but wrong mental model:
- ❌ *"My margin is value. My price is what I capture of that margin."*
- ✅ *"My value is what the customer perceives. My price is what they sacrifice to capture it. The gap is the buy signal."*

The first frame thinks in seller's terms. The second thinks in buyer's terms. Only the second produces high-converting offers.

---

## Application Notes

When evaluating any offer you're designing or selling:

1. **Estimate the perceived value to the customer** (not your cost, not the market price)
2. **Estimate the price**
3. **Calculate the gap** (in absolute dollars *and* as a multiple)
4. **If the multiple is < 5×**, the offer is too weak — either add bonuses or restructure delivery
5. **If the multiple is > 10×**, you may be under-priced

The bigger the multiple, the easier the close, the more referrals, the higher the LTV.
