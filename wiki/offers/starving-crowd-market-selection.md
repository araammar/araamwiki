# Concept: The Starving Crowd — Market Selection
tags: [offers, Hormozi, market-selection, niche, demand, targeting]

## Summary / Definition

Before any pricing, value, or enhancement work matters, **the market matters most**. Hormozi's hierarchy is non-negotiable:

> **Starving Crowd (market) > Offer Strength > Persuasion Skills**

A great offer to the wrong market dies. A normal offer to a great market wins. Every entrepreneur is affected by their market — no skill can outrun a shrinking one.

---

## The Origin Story — The Hot Dog Stand Question

A marketing professor asked his students: *"If you were going to open a hot dog stand, and you could only have one advantage over your competitors . . . which would it be?"*

Students shouted: "Location! Quality! Low prices! Best taste!" The room ran out of answers.

Professor smiled: *"A starving crowd."*

> *"You could have the worst hot dogs, terrible prices, and be in a terrible location, but if you're the only hot dog stand in town and the local college football game breaks out, you're going to sell out."*

That is the value of a starving crowd. At the end of the day, if there's a ton of demand, you can be mediocre at business, have a terrible offer, and have no ability to persuade people — and **still make money**.

The toilet paper shortage at the start of Covid-19 is the same principle. No offer. Atrocious pricing. No sales pitch. But rolls were selling for $100+. Starving crowd.

## The Lloyd Newspaper Cautionary Tale

Lloyd, a friend of Hormozi's, owned a software business that served newspapers for almost a decade. The product was great. The offer was great — he set up digital ad services on newspaper websites with a few clicks, charging only a percentage of revenue he added (rev-share, zero risk). Lloyd was a natural salesman.

But his business kept declining. He tried every angle. He couldn't figure out what was wrong.

The answer was obvious to Hormozi but invisible to Lloyd: **he was selling to newspapers.** His market was shrinking 25% per year.

Lloyd finally accepted it and downsized. Then COVID hit. Same Lloyd. Different market: he started an automated mask manufacturing company, brought cost per mask below the China import price, and within five months was doing **millions per month**.

Same entrepreneur. Same skills. Just a different market.

> *"That's the power of picking the right market."*

## The Four Market Indicators

When picking markets, Hormozi looks for four signals:

### 1. Massive Pain
> *"They must not want, but desperately need, what I am offering."*

Pain can be anything that frustrates people about their lives. Being broke, bad marriage, waiting in line, back pain, ugly smile, overweight. Humans suffer a lot — endless opportunity.

**The degree of pain is proportional to the price you can charge.** When prospects hear the solution to their pain — and inversely what their life looks like without it — they should be drawn to your solution.

Hormozi's sales-team mantra: **"The pain is the pitch."** If you can articulate the pain accurately, prospects almost always buy. See [[pain-is-the-pitch]].

> *"The point of good writing is for the reader to understand. The point of good persuasion is for the prospect to feel understood."*

### 2. Purchasing Power
A friend of Hormozi's had a great system for helping people improve resumes to get more job interviews. He could not get people to pay. Why? **Because they were all unemployed.**

> *"He just forgot a crucial point: your audience needs to be able to afford the service you're charging them for. Make sure your targets have the money, or access to the amount of money, needed to buy your services at the prices you require to make it worth your time."*

### 3. Easy To Target
You can have a perfect market but no way to find them — making a Grand Slam Offer is then impossible.

Easy-to-target signals:
- Avatars that have associations they belong to
- Mailing lists
- Social media groups
- Channels they all watch

If your customers are gathered together somewhere, you can market to them. If they're needles in a haystack, no offer will reach them.

**Clarifying point** (Hormozi explicitly notes): there's no problem wanting to serve rich doctors — they're easy to find. But if your ads are being displayed to nursing students, your offer falls on deaf ears no matter how good it is.

### 4. Growing
Growing markets are **tailwinds** — they make everything move forward faster. Declining markets are **headwinds** — they make every effort harder.

Newspapers had three of four (pain, purchasing power, easy to target) but was shrinking fast. Doesn't matter. Find a tailwind.

## The Three Universal Markets — Health, Wealth, Relationships

Three markets that will always exist because there is always tremendous pain when you lack them:

1. **Health**
2. **Wealth**
3. **Relationships**

**The play:** Find a smaller subgroup *within* one of those buckets that has all four indicators.

### Hormozi's Example
> *"So if I were a relationship expert trying to find my avatar, I'd rather focus on 'second half of life relationship' coaching for old timers than helping college students in relationships."*

Why?
- **Pain**: Senior citizens alone near death suffer more
- **Money**: They have buying power
- **Targeting**: Easy to find
- **Growth**: At time of writing, more people turn 65 each year than 20

## The Three Levers Of Success Hierarchy

Markets are weighted by goodness on a scale: bad → normal → great. The hierarchy moves left to right in order of importance:

> **Starving Crowd > Offer Strength > Persuasion Skills**

A **"great"** rating at a higher-order piece overpowers anything below. A **"normal"** rating moves the buck to the next piece. A **"bad"** rating stops the equation unless overpowered by a "great" higher up.

### Three Concrete Scenarios

**Example 1 (Great market trumps everything):** Even with a bad offer and bad persuasion, you'll make money in a great market. *"If you're on the corner hocking hot dogs when the bars close up at 2am, with mobs of starving drunk folks, you're gonna sell out your hotdogs."*

**Example 2 (Most readers):** Normal market + Grand Slam Offer = tons of money even with bad persuasion. *"This is most people reading this book. That's why I wrote it."*

**Example 3 (Hardest path):** Normal market + normal offer = you need exceptional persuasion skills. Many empires have been built this way, but it's the longest path with the most effort.

> *"Nailing your offer helps you shortcut this path to success. Otherwise, you will just have a normal business that takes exceptional skill to be successful."*

## "Don't Make Me Niche Slap You" — Commit To The Niche

> *"Too often, a newer entrepreneur half-heartedly tries one offer in one market, doesn't make a million dollars, then mistakenly thinks 'this is a bad market.' Most times that's not actually the case. They just haven't found a Grand Slam Offer yet to apply to that market."*

The wrong move: switching from helping dentists to chiropractors because the first didn't pan out. Both are normal markets, both worth billions. **Pick one. Stay.** *"No one can serve two masters."*

> *"All businesses, and all markets, have unpleasant characteristics. The grass is never greener once you get to the other side. If you keep hopping from niche to niche, hoping that the market will solve your problems, you deserve to be niche slapped."*

You must stick with whatever you pick long enough to fail repeatedly. **You will fail until you succeed.** You'll fail far longer if you keep switching, because each time you start over.

## The Resilience Note

> *"If you try one hundred offers, I promise you will succeed. Most people never try anything. Others fail once, then give up."*

> *"Stop personalizing! It's not about you! If your offer doesn't work, it doesn't mean you suck. It means your offer sucks. Big difference. You only suck if you stop trying."*

## When To Broaden (Hormozi's Caveat)

For most people under $10M/year, niching down makes more money.

After $10M, it depends on TAM (Total Addressable Market). A business can only grow to meet its total addressable market.

But getting to $10M/year is already a top **0.4%** achievement (1 in 250 businesses). So for **99.6% of readers below $10M**, it's almost always easier to serve fewer clients more narrowly.

To go beyond $10M, you may need to:
- Go up market
- Go down market
- Move into an adjacent market where your existing services can provide value

**Context:** Many companies expanded to $30M+/year serving a single niche — chiropractors, gyms, plumbers, solar, roofers, salon owners. If you're at $1M–$3M thinking you've capped, you haven't. You just need to be better.

## The "Normal Market" Reality Check

> *"It's unlikely you are going to be in a dying market like the newspaper example. It's also unlikely you're going to be selling toilet paper in COVID (buying frenzy). You'll likely be in a 'normal' market. And that's totally okay."*

There is a fortune to be made within normal markets. The single point: you cannot be in a **bad** market, or nothing will work.

> *"Having a great market is an advantage. But you can be in a normal market that's growing at an average rate and still make crazy money. Every market I have been in has been a normal market. You just don't actually want to be selling ice to eskimos."*

---

## Application Notes

When evaluating any market opportunity, run the four-question audit:
1. Is there massive pain here?
2. Do they have the purchasing power?
3. Can I easily target them?
4. Is the market growing?

If 3/4 score green, you have a normal-to-great market. If 4/4, you have a tailwind that will carry mediocre execution.

If pain is missing → no urgency → no sale.
If money is missing → they want it but can't afford it.
If targeting is missing → can't reach them.
If growth is missing → swimming upstream forever.
