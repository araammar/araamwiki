# Concept: Logical vs Psychological Solutions
tags: [offers, Hormozi, Rory Sutherland, psychology, problem-framing]

## Summary / Definition

> *"Most people naturally try and solve problems using logical solutions. But the logical solutions have usually been tried...because they're logical (it's what everyone would try and do)."*
>
> *"As business owners and entrepreneurs I increasingly approach problems to find psychological solutions, rather than logical ones. Because if there were a logical solution, it probably would have already been solved, thereby eliminating the problem. All that's left are the psychological problems."*

— Alex Hormozi, *$100M Offers* (Pro Tip, Value Equation chapter)

Hormozi explicitly cites this as inspired by **Rory Sutherland**, CMO of Ogilvy Advertising.

---

## The Sutherland Principle

> *"Any fool can sell a product by offering it for a discount, it takes great marketing to sell the same product for a premium."*
>
> — Rory Sutherland, via Hormozi

The job is not always to logically improve the product. The job is to change what the prospect *perceives*. Perception is reality, and perception is shifted with psychology, not with engineering.

---

## The Four Canonical Examples

Hormozi pairs each problem with two solutions — the logical one (which has been tried) and the psychological one (which works better).

### Train Satisfaction
- **Logical solution:** Make trains faster to increase satisfaction (billions of dollars)
- **Psychological solution #1:** Decrease the pain of waiting by adding a dotted-line map showing when the next train arrives (millions of dollars, bigger satisfaction gain)
- **Psychological solution #2:** Pay models to be hostesses on the trip — *"people would wish it took longer to get to their destination!"*

The London Underground actually used the dotted-map solution. The cost was a tiny fraction of making trains faster, but the satisfaction lift was larger. The riders weren't suffering from the train speed — they were suffering from the *uncertainty of waiting*. The map solved the actual pain, which was psychological.

### Elevator Speed
- **Logical solution:** Make the elevator faster (mechanically expensive, often physically impossible)
- **Psychological solution:** Add floor-to-ceiling mirrors so people are distracted staring at themselves and forget how long they were on the elevator

A classic Sutherland example. The elevator wait wasn't the problem — the *experience* of waiting was.

### Pricing
- **Logical solution:** Make it cheaper
- **Psychological solution:** Make fewer of them and raise the price, which causes people to want it more

This is the scarcity mechanic in action — see [[scarcity-playbook]] and [[delicate-dance-of-desire]].

## Why It Matters For Offers

Every part of the [[value-equation]] runs on **perception**, not reality:
- **Perceived** Likelihood of Achievement
- **Perceived** Time Delay
- **Perceived** Effort & Sacrifice

The Grand Slam Offer doesn't just improve outcomes — it improves the prospect's *perception* of likelihood, time, and effort. Often the underlying delivery doesn't change at all.

Hormozi makes this point explicitly:

> *"The Grand Slam Offer only becomes valuable once the prospect **perceives** the increase in likelihood of achievement, **perceives** the decrease in time delay, and **perceives** the decrease in effort and sacrifice."*

You can deliver the most efficient program in the world, but if the prospect doesn't perceive it, the value isn't real to them.

---

## The Strategic Shift

> *"As such, as business owners, it is up to us to communicate these value drivers with clarity to increase the prospect's perception of these realities. The extent to which you answer these questions in the mind of your prospect will determine the value you are creating."*

The implication: **your job is communication, not just delivery.** Even a perfect delivery will fail if the perception isn't built first.

---

## The Inversion — When To Use Logical vs Psychological

The decision rule embedded in Hormozi's framing:

1. **Ask: has anyone tried the logical solution to this problem?**
2. **If yes and it didn't work** → the remaining lever is psychological. Stop iterating on logic.
3. **If no** → try the logical solution first (it's cheaper to execute).

The mistake most business owners make: continuing to iterate on logical improvements (faster delivery, more features, more polish) when the bottleneck is actually psychological (perceived risk, perceived hassle, perceived status loss). At this stage, logical improvements have diminishing returns.

## Practical Application

For any business problem you face, run this audit:

1. What is the actual problem?
2. What's the logical solution?
3. Has it been tried? At what cost?
4. What is the *underlying psychological discomfort* the prospect is actually trying to escape?
5. What is the cheapest, fastest way to make that psychological discomfort go away?

The answer to question 5 is almost always disproportionately more profitable than the answer to question 2.
