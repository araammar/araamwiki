# Concept: The Future of Marketing

## Summary
A synthesis of the emerging trends reshaping marketing practice that are not yet mainstream conversation: visual world building, product as marketing strategy, the 10x creative director, AI in advertising, the clipping economy applied to brands, brand trips and experiential rollouts, and the rise of indie brand storytelling. These trends share a common thread — the collapse of the traditional separation between marketing, product, and media, and the rise of brands that function as cultural producers rather than advertisers.

## Key Trends

### Visual World Building
Marketing is increasingly about constructing a recognizable visual universe around a brand — not just running campaigns or publishing content.

**What it is:** A brand builds a persistent visual identity, recurring characters or lore, and a body of cultural reference that ties everything together. The content and the visual system compound over time into something audiences can participate in and recognize immediately.

**Examples:**
- Aura (running app): Built on X from day one by rapidly integrating internet culture — Drake fonts, Call of Duty killstreak graphics, recurring in-app characters and quotes — into the app's visual system. Users want to share workouts because sharing the app graphic is sharing a piece of cultural ephemera.
- Technology Brothers (TBN) podcast: Rolled out with a consistent visual feel (suits, formal studio, printed tweets), evolved into a live news-format show, created a clippable visual universe that mirrors a major news network. Velocity of media output (live hours daily) combined with visual consistency created a recognizable world within months.
- Red Bull (original): Replaced an entire category of broadcast TV with owned media. The world of action sports, extreme athletes, and impossible events is the Red Bull brand world.

**Brand implication:** World building requires someone who understands how things move online to be present in product development, packaging, and release planning meetings. The small decisions — color schemes, postability of an object, recurring visual motifs — compound over time into cultural distinction.

### Product as Marketing
The most powerful campaigns in 2025–2026 are products designed to be marketing. Viral SKUs — items that exist primarily to generate conversation, earn coverage, and drive attention to the actual product line — are no longer a fringe tactic. They are being built into product development roadmaps at serious companies.

**What it is:** A product (or product variation) is developed with virality as a primary design criterion. The product may sell modest volume, but it anchors conversations, drives earned media, and funnels attention toward the rest of the line.

**Examples:**
- Skims head wrap: Released with Anthony Hopkins reprising his Silence of the Lambs character. Unlikely to sell significant volume, but purely a marketing event built into the product roadmap.
- Progresso "soup drops": Real product, pure marketing stone. Made them, they were purchasable, conversation value massively exceeded unit economics.
- Gustaf Westman x IKEA meatball tray: More conversation around this item than the collaboration itself. The viral item drives demand for everything else in the collaboration.
- David Bar cod brick: A literal brick of cod released to prove the protein density of their bar. Immaculately art-directed campaign. The stunt is the product; the real product is the bar.

**The integration challenge:** Social media people need to be in the product development meetings, not just the marketing meetings. The packaging, the color, the scale of an object, its "postability" — these are now design criteria, not afterthoughts.

**The execution timeline:** Companies that got on this early are now 2–3 product cycles ahead. The roadmap requires lead time. Starting now means you'll be executing these in a year. Waiting means you're perennially behind the brands who planned in 2023–2024.

### The 10x Creative Director
Some brands have begun finding creative directors (or photographers/directors with their own cultural cachet) whose involvement is itself a quality signal to audiences — and whose aesthetic is distinctive enough to make the brand's content instantly recognizable.

**What it is:** A creative director whose personal following, visual signature, and cultural reputation multiplies the impact of every campaign they touch. They often have their own social platform and followers who follow the creative director as much as they follow the brand.

**Examples:**
- Nadia Lee Cohen x Skims: Responsible for the Cavali Skims campaign. Her medium-format film aesthetic is immediately recognizable. Her 1M+ followers and personal brand add reach beyond what Skims could achieve alone.
- Sarah (creative director at David Bar): A protein bar brand should not require a creative director, but David's aesthetic (the gold wrapper, the Greek refinement) and the immaculate campaign art direction for the cod bar launch demonstrate that creative direction is a brand multiplier even in CPG.
- Sandy (K-pop art director): Found through K-pop visual direction work, now doing art direction for mainstream fashion and beauty campaigns — working with brands that would otherwise compete with each other.

**The broader ecosystem:** There is a whole cottage industry of creative directors, photographers, and visual thinkers who can be multipliers for brands — shooting it themselves, directing a crew, or at least conceptualizing what the aesthetic needs to be. These people are in high demand and often accessible via social media.

**The 10x concept applied internally:** Even without hiring a famous creative director, brands can ask: does our lead visual decision-maker have a point of view that's distinctive enough to function as a brand signature? If not, that's the hire.

### AI in Advertising
The debate about whether AI-generated ads are acceptable is over. The watershed moment: **Koshi** (a supplement/AG1 competitor), with creator **PJ Asuro**, produced a fully AI-generated hype-reel style video campaign in two days, aired it on television, and went viral — outperforming most human-created campaigns in the same period. Participating in the "AI or not AI" debate at this point is a waste of time. The result is already here.

**The current state:**
- AI is most effective for hype-reel style content, otherworldly scenarios, and rapid concept visualization
- Consistent characters across clips remain technically difficult
- Transparency (disclosed AI use) is increasingly expected but not yet legally required in most markets
- Generational adoption is nonlinear — Gen Alpha and younger Gen Z don't register AI as distinct from other content creation tools

**The brand position:** Don't debate whether to use AI. Decide where it adds leverage. Use it to generate rapid concept iteration and test creative directions before committing production budget. Use it where the "otherworldly" aesthetic is appropriate (spectacle content, sci-fi scenarios, hyperbolic USP demonstrations).

**The risk:** Over-relying on AI for content that requires human authenticity — testimonials, expert commentary, founder storytelling. AI-generated hooks on top of human-delivered substance is a viable model; AI-generated substance with no human presence is where conversion fails.

### The Clipping Economy for Brands
See also: [[Internet Splintering]]

The same bounty-per-thousand-views infrastructure that built streaming personalities can be applied to brand content libraries.

**How it works for brands:**
1. Build a compelling content library (product demos, campaign footage, founder content, behind-the-scenes)
2. Set up a bounty program via Whop or similar platforms
3. Clippers self-select and compete to produce the highest-performing cuts
4. Brand pays only on performance (CPM-equivalent)
5. Best-performing clippers get hired directly or put on retainer

**Why most brands aren't doing it:** Their content libraries aren't compelling enough to clip (no USP, no story, no visual interest). Building a clippable library requires solving the upstream content problem first.

**The alpha:** Most brands don't know what bounty programs are. The brands that establish a clipping program while the majority are still doing organic posting have a significant distribution advantage.

### Brand Trips and Experiential Rollouts
Gathering influencers (and customers) at a real-world event or destination to generate concentrated, algorithm-amplified social content. The model: attach the brand to an event or create its own event, give influencers memorable material to document, let their audiences and the event's native algorithm compound the reach.

**Why trips work:**
- Multiple influencers posting from the same place/event triggers algorithm recognition — their audiences overlap, and the algorithm shows content from "the trip" to people who follow any of the participants
- Attaching to events with their own algorithmic momentum (US Open, Expo West, fashion week) adds a third multiplier to the brand + influencer multiplier
- Invitation to a notable experience generates inherent social proof — being chosen signals brand credibility

**Examples:**
- Graza (olive oil) at the US Open: Influencers on a brand trip to the US Open documenting the experience. The US Open's own content flywheel amplifies the Graza presence.
- Hotel Lobby Candles: Influencers on a quasi-private jet to the Hamptons. The jet is a spectacle; the candles are present throughout. A candle brand generating jet content.
- Water Boy: Brand trip with influencers and customers. Even when the trip had messy moments, it generated awareness that no equivalent ad spend could replicate.
- Cozy Earth's Bedrock Challenge: 530,000 likes, 105,000 shares on a single video from a manufactured challenge event with influencer participants.

**The economics:** A $150,000 experiential investment generating 500K+ shares across dozens of influencer posts outperforms a $150,000 ad campaign. The comparison is not the sticker price — it's the attention generated per dollar.

### The Rise of Indie Brand Storytelling
Small, founder-led brands that tell their authentic story on social — documented through vlogs, carousels, and real customer interactions — are consistently outperforming corporate accounts with significant agency budgets.

**What makes indie storytelling work:**
- Genuine narrative stakes (a person who quit their job to start something, the actual journey of building)
- Documentary authenticity (real people, real interactions, real locations)
- Recurring presence (daily or near-daily content, consistent cast of characters)

**Examples:**
- Bad Ombres: Hank quit his job to start a frozen bean and cheese burrito company. Tells the story on social in vlogs and carousels. 100K follower business that sells out of product.
- Nitro Bar (coffee): Personality and idea-driven content — "how we'd make a drink if Lana Del Rey came in." Conceptual, low-fi, narrative-driven.
- Cheese Store of Beverly Hills: One post a day of iPhone-shot customer interactions and cheese discussions. Outperforms corporate accounts with professional production.

**The implication for larger brands:** The authentic storytelling mode that indie brands naturally produce is the hardest thing for large corporations to replicate. The structural barriers — approval processes, legal review, brand safety requirements — prevent the kind of rapid, candid publishing that makes indie content resonate. Large brands that want this authenticity need to create genuinely autonomous content pods with minimal approval overhead.

## Synthesis: What These Trends Share
All of these trends converge on the same insight: the distinction between product, marketing, and media has collapsed. The brands winning in this era:
- Design products to be marketed (viral SKUs)
- Make their visual systems into worlds (world building)
- Use creative directors as brand amplifiers (10x creative director)
- Distribute via ecosystem, not just owned channels (clipping, brand trips)
- Tell stories that make audiences participants, not spectators (indie storytelling)

The brands losing are those still treating marketing as a separate department that activates after the product is finished.
