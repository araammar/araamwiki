# Concept: Writing Simply
tags: [copywriting, writing, clarity, editing, rewriting, structure]

## Summary / Definition

Writing simply is not about dumbing things down. It's about removing everything that doesn't serve the reader. The core insight: you cannot write simply — you can only *rewrite* simply. Simple writing is the result of editing, not first drafts.

Harry Dry has had no paragraph longer than two lines in his newsletter since March 2021. His newsletter has 100K+ subscribers with engagement rates no other newsletter matches.

## Why It Matters

Complexity loses readers. Every unnecessary word is a small tax. Every bloated paragraph makes the reader work harder. The reader is on their phone, on a train, half-paying attention. Your job is to make it effortless for them to stay.

Simple writing is also a diagnostic tool: if you can't write it simply, you probably don't understand it well enough yet.

## How It Works / The Mechanism

### Kaplan's Law of Words

> "Any words that aren't working for you are working against you."

Not neutral — actively against. Every word earns its place or should be cut.

Harry's corollary — Harry's Law of Words:
> "You aren't taking Kaplan's Law seriously enough."

People say "I write simply" and still have three sentences where one would do. Kaplan's Law is stricter than you think. Apply it harder than feels comfortable.

### The "And" Warning Sign

The word "and" on a landing page is almost never good. It signals that you're trying to say two things at once. The strength of an idea is inversely proportional to its scope.

HuDenim's tagline: **"We make jeans."** 
Not: "We make jeans, t-shirts, socks, and accessories, and we have dressing rooms."

Every "and" dilutes. One thing, said well, beats five things said adequately. Same principle applies to album covers: Velvet Underground's banana, Pink Floyd's prism, Bowie's lightning bolt on Aladdin Sane — one image, one idea. His worst album cover? Seven disparate images. No focus.

### The Paragraph Burrito Test

A good paragraph is like a burrito you can throw to someone — it won't come apart in the air. The sentences inside should add to more than the sum of their parts.

Test: pull one sentence out of a paragraph. If the paragraph still makes sense without it, the sentence probably shouldn't have been there. Kaplan's Law applies to ideas too, not just words.

### Short Paragraphs: The Monkey Butt Rule

Short paragraphs are like monkey butts — easy to swing between.

People read on their phones, on the train, in distractions. You're not competing against other writing. You're competing against everything else in their environment. Short paragraphs give the reader a handhold to jump from.

Harry's rule (since March 2021): **no paragraph longer than two lines.**

Three reasons:
1. Short paragraphs are easier to scan and swing between
2. If a paragraph runs to three lines, there's a good chance he isn't explaining himself as well as he could be — so he cuts
3. He likes giving lines room to breathe

### Rewriting, Not Writing

You can't write simply on the first pass. The first draft is about getting the idea out. Simple writing only appears through editing.

Harry's technique: write 4–5 versions of the same important paragraph or headline. Don't evaluate versions while writing them — just write each one differently. Then look at all five together. Show them to someone: "Which of these is the best?" You'll get answers like "That sentence from version 2, in the structure of version 3." That's useful feedback. One version in isolation only gets "I like it" or "I don't."

The freedom to write bad versions creates permission to try something different. The best version sometimes comes from the rewrite you thought was worst.

### Structure: Dividing Lines and Parallelism

Structure is clarity applied at the macro level. It's how you make a long or complex idea holdable.

The method: find the dividing line. What are the two parts? Name them with parallel language.

Example: "The first step to getting 10,000 readers is getting 100 readers." That one sentence creates the structure for an entire essay:
- Part 1: Things that don't scale (0 → 100 readers)
- Part 2: Momentum (next 9,900 readers)

Parallelism in headings: "The corporate way / The copywriting way" works. "The corporate way / Learn to write" doesn't — no symmetry. The reader needs the structure to feel consistent.

The 1971 paper "That's Interesting": take two things people think are different — show they're one. Take one thing — show it's actually two. Structure often comes from this simple reveal.

## Examples of Writing Simply Done Right

**HuDenim: "We make jeans."**
Full stop. Megan Markle shops there. They don't need to say more.

**Hinge: "The dating app designed to be deleted."**
Six words. Three Ds (designed/dating/deleted — alliteration doing structural work). Nothing wasted.

**1,000 songs in your pocket.**
Not "1,000 songs in your MP3 player with 1GB of storage." One punchy, visual phrase.

**Economist: "I've never read The Economist. Management trainee, aged 42."**
No logo, no subheader, no picture. Two lines create a complete narrative arc.

## What to Steal / Application

- After every draft, run Kaplan's Law: read each word and ask "is this working for me or against me?" Cut mercilessly.
- Whenever you write "and," ask if you're trying to do two things at once. You probably are. Pick one.
- Write 4-5 versions of your most important lines. Compare them and pick the best.
- Break paragraphs at two lines. If it feels unnatural, the sentence you just cut probably didn't need to be there.
- Find the structure by finding the dividing line: what are the two halves of this idea? Name both with parallel language.
- When stuck, ask: what's the single thing I'm trying to say? Write only that.
