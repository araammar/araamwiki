# Concept: Anchor Customer Underwriting

## Summary / Definition
A product launch and SKU expansion discipline: don't begin production of a new SKU until a committed retail or distribution customer (the "anchor customer") has confirmed they will take that product. The committed purchase order — or equivalent confirmed intent — underwrites the production risk before manufacturing begins, transforming inventory builds from speculative bets into demand-pull decisions.

Ross Mackay: *"We won't press yes on our orange flavor until the retailer has said, 'We will launch orange in June.' I go back to the manufacturer and say press go."*

## Why It Matters
Inventory is cash. Cash runs the business. For early-stage CPG brands, the most common path to failure is not bad product — it's cash tied up in inventory that doesn't sell at the velocity projected. Every new SKU is a bet. Anchor customer underwriting converts that bet into a much higher-probability event by securing demand before committing to supply.

In beverage specifically, minimum order quantities (MOQs) on printed cans run into the millions of units. Committing to that run without a retailer commitment is a significant financial risk. One unsold run can create a cash crisis.

## How It Works / The Mechanism

### The Standard Problem
The default early-stage product development cycle:
1. Founder has a product idea
2. Manufacturer is engaged, samples are developed
3. MOQ is committed and production begins
4. Product arrives in warehouse
5. Brand tries to sell it through D2C, hoping retail interest follows
6. Inventory sits (some moves, some doesn't)
7. Cash is tied up, new product development is constrained

This is an inventory-push model. It puts the risk entirely on the brand's balance sheet before there is any demand signal.

### The Anchor Customer Gate
The revised process:
1. Founder develops the product concept and sample
2. Retailer is engaged with the concept as part of normal buyer relationship management
3. Buyer evaluates and signals intent: "We will launch this in June" or "We're interested for fall reset"
4. Only upon retailer confirmation does the brand go back to the manufacturer and press go
5. Production begins with a known demand destination

This is a demand-pull model. The retailer's commitment converts the production risk from speculative to contracted.

**Note:** This doesn't require a formal purchase order in all cases. Strong buyer intent — especially from an existing retail partner who has already placed orders — is sometimes sufficient to de-risk the production decision. The key is having a real, validated signal from a real customer before committing cash.

### Why Founders Struggle With This
The temptation to produce ahead of demand is real:
- Innovation feels exciting — founders want to see new products in the world
- D2C can absorb some inventory even without retail commitment
- Lead times (often 12–16 weeks for beverage) mean you have to make decisions before you have certainty

The discipline is in treating "I think this will sell" as insufficient to trigger production, and "a retailer has committed to launch this" as the trigger.

### The Founder Veto Exception
In a well-run stage gate process, the standard decision is rule-based: only produce with anchor customer commitment. But founders sometimes have strong conviction that a product is strategically important enough to build ahead of proven demand.

The veto should be rare (once a year, not once a quarter) and must meet one of two criteria:
1. **The product is a category-changing innovation** that no one in the market is paying attention to — a strategic bet on a whitespace
2. **The product cements brand positioning** with the core ICP even if its commercial ceiling is small

Cadence's example of the carbohydrate powder: small market, won't be a $100M SKU, but signals to the core endurance athlete ICP that Cadence is a serious performance nutrition brand. That positioning value — protecting the brand's credibility in its founding community — can justify the production risk.

### The Self-Financing Founder Pitch
Before formal retail relationships exist, the same logic applies to manufacturing relationships. The early-stage founder must convince the manufacturer to invest time and samples in a brand that hasn't yet proven scale.

Ross Mackay's early approach: "I just told the manufacturer I was way bigger than I was and was in stealth mode and about to launch here and all these exciting customers. They trusted me and were willing to work with me, knowing I was going to bet on myself."

This is not lying (or at worst, it's a "white lie" of painting a credible future as if it exists). It's using the founder's conviction and credibility as the underwriting for a relationship that the numbers alone couldn't justify yet. The manufacturer is making a bet on the founder, not the current financials.

The responsibility: if you've painted a big picture, you have to deliver it. The manufacturer who trusted Ross Mackay saw everything Ross said materialize — which created stronger trust and better terms for subsequent rounds.

### Application to the Distributor/Broker Relationship
The same principle applies to distribution:
- Distributors are box movers. They won't sell your product. They'll move product that's already been sold.
- Brokers are sales agents — but their effort follows the brands that are already proven, not the ones that need selling.

The correct sequence: Win the anchor retail customer yourself first. Then the distributor and broker will happily take your business — because you've already done the hard part.

Don't engage brokers before you have the anchor customer. "Go win the end customer yourself. Do whatever it takes to get that product to that customer. Once you want to optimize, turn to the broker and say: we have this customer, now you can have them."

## What to Steal / Application

**For any CPG brand expanding SKUs:** Before the next product launch meeting, ask: do we have a committed retail buyer for this product? If not, who are we making it for? What's the minimum signal we need before pressing go?

**For first-time manufacturers of a new SKU:** Reduce MOQ exposure by going with a sleeve can (vs. printed can), a smaller initial run, or a co-pack partner that can produce smaller volumes before you commit to the full branded run.

**For beverage founders specifically:** The printed can is the premium signal — but it carries 5–10x the MOQ of a sleeve can. Know what's "must have" vs. "nice to have" before committing to the premium format at scale.

**For the founder pitch:** Until you have a Walmart PO in hand, you're selling the future. Paint the future credibly. Then deliver it. Each delivery compounds your credibility with manufacturers, distributors, and buyers.
