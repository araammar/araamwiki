# Concept: Show Me You Know Me (SMYKM)
tags: [cold-email, copywriting, personalization, B2B, subject-line, outbound, executive-outreach]
source: ydsMxs2yeos — Sam McKenna, Apollo Academy Cold Email Course

## Summary / Definition

A cold email philosophy built on one principle: prove to the recipient that you specifically researched them before anything else. The subject line is the primary vehicle — it should reference something so personal and specific that it makes zero sense to anyone except the person receiving it. When done right, executives don't feel sold to. They feel seen.

**Creator:** Sam McKenna, CEO of #samsales Consulting

## Why It Matters

Industry average cold email performance:
- Open rate: **6%**
- Reply rate: **0.9%** (not even a full percentage point)

SMYKM average performance:
- Open rate: **43%**
- Reply rate: **20%**

The gap is not explained by better offers or better products — it's entirely explained by whether the recipient feels like the email was written for them specifically, or written for anyone who fits a job title.

## How It Works / The Mechanism

### The Core Test

Before sending any cold email, apply the SMYKM test to the subject line:

> "Does this subject line make sense to anyone other than this specific person?"

If yes → it's not personal enough. Rewrite it.
If no → you did it correctly.

The subject line should be incomprehensible to anyone outside of the recipient. It should reference something only they would recognize about themselves.

---

### Layer 1 — The Subject Line

**The most important part of the email.** You have seconds to capture attention from a CEO, VP, or executive whose inbox is flooded with generic "quick question" and "available to talk?" messages.

**What makes a SMYKM subject line:**
- References a personal fact about them: where they're from, a school they attended, a club or fraternity, a pet they've posted about, a restaurant they mention online
- Makes zero sense out of context
- Creates enough curiosity that they have to open it to understand what it means

**Real example from Sam:**
- Chief Logistics Officer of Tori Burch (multi-billion dollar company)
- Subject line: designed around something specific to that CLO
- Result: email replied to in under 10 hours
- The CLO said: *"Your email stood out in a sea of emails that I get every single day."*

**Sam's own example subject line:**
`Switzerland + liip cheeseburger + [company name]`
- Sam is from Switzerland (born near Geneva)
- Lives in Washington DC and talks about a specific cheeseburger restaurant
- This subject line would only land with Sam — and it would definitely get her to open it

**Formatting tips:**
- Use `+` signs to break up the subject line — it visually separates personal references from company context
- Shorter is not necessarily better — don't constrain to under 4 words
- Structure: [Personal reference 1] + [Personal reference 2] + [Company/context]

**The scalability move:**
- Find executives who share a trait (same fraternity, same alma mater, same city, same conference)
- Use the same subject line template across all of them
- Each recipient still feels like it was written only for them because the personal reference is real

---

### Layer 2 — The First Sentence (Preview Text)

Subject line + first sentence = preview text = what determines if the email gets read.

**Two approaches:**

**Option A — The Introduction:**
`"Hi [Name], we have yet to be properly introduced, but I'm Sam McKenna and..."`

Counter-intuitive: conventional wisdom says don't introduce yourself in the first sentence. But when paired with a researched SMYKM subject line, "we have yet to be properly introduced" implies *we should have been* — it signals that you already know them, not that you're a cold stranger.

**Option B — Straight into the personal moment:**
`"[Name], I saw your recent post about your adorable furry companion Sprinkles and..."`
Then tie it authentically back to yourself.

High-level executives frequently post about their dogs — this is a well-documented pattern and a reliable opening. The key: you need an authentic personal connection, not a forced one.

---

### Layer 3 — The Value Proposition

**The most misunderstood part of cold emails.**

> Your value proposition is not what you do. It is the challenge you solve for that specific buyer.

Most cold emails state what the company does. That is a marketing email, not a sales email. The buyer doesn't care what you do — they care whether you can solve a problem they actually have.

**Counterintuitive position: longer is better.**

Conventional cold email advice says keep it short. Sam disagrees — and the data backs her:

> "We have to convince our buyer that time with us will be well spent. When you state something specifically that you can solve for them, then think: what is the next big objection? What is the buyer thinking? What's their rebuttal?"

**The preemptive objection technique:**

1. State the specific challenge you solve (not what you do)
2. Anticipate the most common objection they'll have
3. Address that objection directly in the same email — before they say it

**Real example (Sam's company — LinkedIn Sales Navigator training):**

> *"If you're anything like our clients, you see your usage rates of LinkedIn Sales Navigator hover around 5% of your licenses. We can train your teams and bring that number up."*

Most common objection: *"We already have a customer success manager at LinkedIn."*

Preemptive response built into the email:

> *"You likely have a customer success manager at LinkedIn — but rather than having them teach you what buttons to push, our team will teach you how to use this platform to sell in the same way that [our trainer] did when she was an executive at LinkedIn for her teams."*

Effect: the buyer thinks *"Huh — maybe there IS something different here."* The objection is gone before it was ever stated.

---

### Layer 4 — The CTA

**What not to do:**

| CTA Format | Why It Fails |
|-----------|-------------|
| Calendar link (Calendly) | Presumptuous, slightly rude, puts the burden on them to fit your schedule |
| "Do you have 15–20 min tomorrow?" | Too presumptuous on timeline; creates friction |
| "Would Monday at 1pm work?" | Too specific — they say no to one time slot and now you're chasing |

All of these fail for the same reason: they're optimized for your convenience, not theirs. Executives notice.

**What works:**

> *"Do you have time over the next week or two to learn more? Let me know what works for you and I'll send a calendar invite along accordingly."*

Why this works:
- Gives them a wide window (week or two) — no false urgency
- Puts the scheduling burden on you, not them ("I'll send the invite")
- Nobody else writes CTAs that look like this — it stands out by being considerate
- You maintain control: you send the invite on their terms, but you control the timing once they say yes

---

### The Full SMYKM Email Structure

```
Subject: [Personal fact] + [Personal fact] + [Company/context]
  → Makes zero sense to anyone except them

First sentence:
  Option A: "Hi [Name], we have yet to be properly introduced..."
  Option B: Straight into the personal moment from the subject line

Value proposition:
  → The specific challenge you solve for them (not what you do)
  → Preempt the most common objection immediately after

CTA:
  "Do you have time over the next week or two? 
   Let me know what works and I'll send the invite accordingly."
```

## Contrast with Other Frameworks

**vs. Triple Tap (Lee Gjenj):**
- Lee: 2–3 sentences max, one-word CTA, shorter always better
- Sam: longer is better when you can justify the length; softer scheduling CTA; focus on preempting objections in the body
- Both agree: subject line must not signal a sale; personalization is the primary lever
- The approaches serve different contexts — Lee's is optimized for high-volume B2B cold email at scale; Sam's is optimized for high-value executive outreach where each email is more customized

## What to Steal / Application

- Run every subject line through the SMYKM test: does it make sense to anyone except this specific person? If yes, it's too generic.
- Research personal facts: LinkedIn activity, Twitter/X posts, photos, publications, school affiliations, previous companies. Look for what they post about outside of work.
- The `+` sign format is a simple way to string personal references together in a subject line without making it feel like a run-on sentence
- Preemptive objection handling belongs in every email where you know what the #1 pushback will be — don't wait for them to say it
- Replace calendar link CTAs with the "next week or two" open-window format — it's genuinely more respectful and it differentiates you
