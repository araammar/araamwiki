# Concept: Shelf Visibility Design

## Summary / Definition
The practice of designing product packaging specifically for the retail shelf environment — accounting for the physical constraints of how a product will actually be displayed, what portion of the packaging will be visible, and what information a shopper can process in a 2-second glance.

Dom Iacovone describes the Bum Energy can redesign process — iterating on 400 renders with Ross Mackay — as one of the highest-leverage packaging decisions in RAW/Bum's history. The key insight: **everything below the shelf blocker is invisible.**

## Why It Matters
DTC brands design packaging for a screen: the full product is visible, zoomed in, on a white background. In retail, the product sits in a refrigerated unit with a blocker bar at the front that obscures the bottom third or half of the product.

Most DTC-first brands that enter retail fail to adapt their packaging to this constraint. Their brand name is in the middle, the flavor is at the bottom, and the shopper just sees an unmarked top portion of the can.

Dom: *"If you look at this can now, what do you see? You see everything you need to see — the name of the company, the flavor, all the call outs. Because this is what shows. Everything under here is useless."*

## How It Works / The Mechanism

**The retail shelf constraint:**
1. Products sit in an angled shelf unit
2. A "blocker" bar at the front of the shelf covers everything from roughly mid-can down
3. What a shopper actually sees: the top 40–60% of the packaging
4. Design must communicate brand + flavor + key callouts entirely within this visible zone

**The Bum Energy redesign:**
Before entering retail, Bum Energy redesigned the can with Ross Mackay. Changes made:
- **Brand name moved to top** — visible above the blocker
- **Flavor name moved to top** — color-coded band at the very top of the can to indicate flavor
- **Key callouts (functional claims)** concentrated in the visible top portion
- **Bottom of can intentionally empty** — because it's not seen; no wasted design real estate

Result: the redesign dramatically improved sell-through because shoppers could instantly identify the brand and flavor without picking up the can.

**The 2-second rule:**
In retail, a shopper walking the aisle has 2 seconds to process your product. In that window they need to understand: who made this, what flavor/variant is this, why should I choose it?

Every element above the blocker must answer these three questions.

**The DTC-to-retail translation problem:**
DTC packaging is designed for screens. Consumers zoom in, they can read everything, the brand context is established before they arrive. Retail shoppers have no brand context — they're comparing your product against 20 others on a shelf without any prior knowledge.

This is why packaging that works online often fails at retail: the information hierarchy is wrong for the shelf environment.

**Sell-through as a packaging metric:**
Shelf velocity (units sold per week per store) is the metric retailers track. If velocity falls below their hurdle rate, they reduce your shelf space or delist you. Packaging that communicates better at shelf directly improves velocity — and therefore your retail relationship.

## What to Steal / Application
- **Test your packaging in a mock retail environment before finalizing.** Put the product on a shelf blocker. Step back. What do you see in 2 seconds?
- **Move the most critical information (brand name, variant, #1 claim) to the top third of the package.**
- **Color-code variants** so shoppers can distinguish flavors instantly at shelf without reading.
- **Design the bottom of the package for DTC/e-commerce close-ups** — it doesn't need to serve the retail environment.
- **Do this before retail entry, not after.** Dom spent 3 days iterating 400 renders. The earlier you start, the cheaper the iteration.

## Real Data: Packaging Change = 3x Sales

Ross Mackay (Cadence) provides one of the most concrete data points on packaging's direct impact on sell-through:

> *"Our first can was just like the minimal D2C can, but it didn't work on shelf. We saw that data. When we launched the new can — same liquid — sales were three times. It was literally a packaging decision."*

The change: added a prominent half-inch colored rim for flavor identification, moved key USPs (no sugar, no caffeine) to the visible zone, and redesigned for instant shelf legibility. The liquid didn't change. The brand didn't change. The packaging changed — and velocity tripled.

This is the single most powerful case study for why shelf visibility investment is not optional for brands entering retail.

**Additional tension from regulatory constraints:**
Approximately 35% of a beverage can's surface area is non-negotiable: barcode, UPC, FDA-required callouts, fluid ounce designations. Brands fight to maximize brand expression in the remaining 65%. The art direction battle is real — QA and regulatory requirements constantly clash with design intent.
