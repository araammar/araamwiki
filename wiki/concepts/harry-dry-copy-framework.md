# Concept: Harry Dry Copy Framework
tags: [copywriting, advertising, marketing, copy-testing, positioning, concrete-language]

## Summary / Definition

Harry Dry (founder of Marketing Examples) distills good copywriting into three questions and three production principles. If your copy answers yes to all three questions, you're onto something. If it fails all three, it's rubbish. The three questions are the fastest diagnostic tool for ad copy, headlines, taglines, subject lines — anything written to persuade.

## The Three-Question Copy Test

Every piece of copy should pass three tests:

### 1. Can I visualize it?
If you can't see it, it's not there yet.

The zoom-in exercise: draw a diagonal line on paper. Write the abstract word at the top ("seamless transition"). Keep asking "what do I actually mean?" and descend the line until you reach a concrete, drawable object ("charging pitbull"). You remember what you can visualize. You forget what you can't.

> "Seamless transition" — nobody remembers. "Charging pitbull" — everybody does.

Metonymy: substitute a literal term with a more visual/punchy word. Apple's iPod — they didn't say "1,000 songs in your media player." They said "1,000 songs in your **pocket**." Pocket is visual, surprising, punchy. The sentence doesn't literally make sense — and that's the point.

### 2. Can I falsify it?

"You can only point, not talk."

Instead of making claims, point to evidence. The blind date analogy: anyone can say someone is "intelligent and good-looking." But "reads on the tube" and "6'2"" are falsifiable — true or false, you can check. They carry more weight because they're checkable.

Applied to advertising: don't say your product is innovative. Point to the graph. Point to the customer using it. Point to the fact only you can claim.

> "You can't just point at Warren Buffett and say 'he's smart.' Point at his portfolio."

Volkswagen: instead of saying "we're a reliable car," they said "Your car has five numbers on the speedometer. Volvo has six. One could get the impression the people who made your car lack a little confidence." Visual. Falsifiable. Competitor can't sign it.

### 3. Can nobody else say it?

Never write an ad a competitor can sign.

The rule forces you to dig deeper into what's actually unique. Can only Corvette say "they don't write songs about Volvos"? Yes. Can only New Balance say "worn by supermodels in London and dads in Ohio"? Yes. That tension — supermodels + dads — is both falsifiable AND passes the "can nobody else say it" test.

David Kitson (mediocre footballer) outsold Beckham, Lampard, Gerrard, and Owen's books combined. How? He signed his book as "The Secret Footballer" — anonymous insider expose of Premier League life. Nobody else could say it. Right positioning makes an inferior product outsell a superior one.

## The Three-Piece Production Framework

Beyond the test, Harry's actual writing process runs on three pieces:

**Piece 1 — Who are you talking to?**
Context determines everything. Snapchat spent $7M on a Super Bowl ad aimed at people who'd use Snapchat — the average Super Bowl viewer is 39. The Yellow Canary coffee shop beat competitors with "Coffee and pastry = £5" because tourists walking past aren't reading menus, they're making 1-second decisions. Write for the moment the reader will actually read it — not for the ideal reader in a vacuum.

**Piece 2 — Do you have something to say?**
You need something to say beyond "buy this." It comes from believing something — a vision, a thing you think is wrong with the world. Harry runs Marketing Examples because he believes copywriting is the #1 marketing skill and that all the existing ways to learn it are boring and academic. That belief generates an infinite supply of things worth writing.

**Piece 3 — Say it well.**
This is what most people think copywriting is — saying things rhythmically, visually, persuasively. It's actually the last piece. You need pieces 1 and 2 first.

## Key Ad Examples (and What to Steal)

**Hinge: "The dating app designed to be deleted."**
Near-perfect. Alliteration (designed/deleted). Juxtaposition (who makes an app that wants to be deleted?). Nobody else can say it — Hinge owns that phrase now. Implies: I'll meet someone and live happily ever after.

**Economist: "I've never read The Economist. Management trainee, aged 42."**
Neil French rule: if you can do an ad with just *one* of the four elements (header, subheader, picture, logo), you're onto a winner. This ad has no subheader, no picture, no logo — the "I've never read The Economist" IS the logo. The visual hierarchy creates a story: you read the first line → confusion → second line reveals the story. Economist red = instant brand recognition without the logo.

**DeBeers: "How can you make two months salary last forever? The diamond engagement ring."**
Mary Gerity, 1948. Juxtaposition: two months salary (finite, painful) vs. forever (infinite). They shouldn't go together — and that's exactly why it works. Result: 85% of engagement rings are now diamonds. A campaign created an entire consumer behavior.

**Athletic Greens: "You're going to need a smaller cabinet."**
Pain point without stating the pain. The vitamin-taker immediately pictures their crowded supplement shelf. Passes the 2-second test for the target audience — and that's fine if outsiders don't get it.

**"The sport sedan for people who inherited brains instead of wealth."**
Identity positioning. Makes the buyer feel smart for choosing it. Everyone wants to be the person with brains, not just wealth. For a car that isn't cool, this is the only honest argument available — and it works because it makes the buyer feel validated.

**Cybertruck (Elon's version): "Better truck than an F-150, faster than a Porsche 911."**
Compare an unknown product to known reference points. But "better" is weak. "Tougher" is more concrete, shorter, eliminates the word "truck." Tougher than an F-150. Faster than a Porsche 911. Parallel structure. Only the Cybertruck can say this.

**Tesla Model S (The Ad Professor): "It takes 3.1 seconds to read this ad. The same time it takes a Model S to go from 0 to 60."**
Falsifiable (3.1 seconds). Precise. Breaks the fourth wall. Joe Sugarman technique: line one creates a question → line two completes it. You can't disengage after line one.

## What to Steal / Application

- Run the three-question test on every piece of copy before publishing. Three yeses = ship it. Two or fewer = rewrite.
- When stuck on abstract words, run the zoom-in exercise: keep asking "what do I actually mean?" until you reach something drawable.
- Instead of describing benefits, point to evidence: the graph, the fact, the competitor's failure.
- Find the one true thing only you can say. Audit what's unique about your product, your brand, your story — then build the copy outward from that.
- Juxtaposition creates attention: two things that shouldn't coexist ("designed to be deleted", "two months salary / forever", "supermodels and dads").
- When you have a lame product, you don't compete on features — you compete on identity (who the buyer becomes by choosing it).
- The One Mississippi Two Mississippi test: if it takes more than 2 seconds to get, the copy isn't ready.
