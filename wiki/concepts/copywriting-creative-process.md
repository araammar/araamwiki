# Concept: Copywriting Creative Process (Harry Dry Method)
tags: [copywriting, creative-process, iteration, standards, design, advertising]

## Summary / Definition

Harry Dry's creative process for writing ads, landing pages, and newsletters. Not a formula — a repeatable set of moves that produces work that couldn't have been written in one sitting. The process typically involves 20–25 rewrites over 1–3 days. The finished ad looks simple; the process that made it is not.

## Why It Matters

Most people think copywriting is sitting down and "coming up with something." Harry's process proves it's closer to sculpture — you start with a rough block and remove everything that shouldn't be there. The difference between a mediocre ad and a great one is usually 20 rewrites, not 20 IQ points.

## How It Works / The Mechanism

### Step 1 — Start with a Seed

A seed is a fact, quote, observation, or piece of data that contains a story. Not an idea for the ad — just something true that makes you think "there's something here."

Example: Harry read a tweet — "The difference between 1% and 2% is not just 1%, it's 100%." He wrote it down. That was the seed for a campaign about copywriting's ROI.

Seeds often come from:
- Facts with surprising implications ("Marketers spend ~22,000 hours writing in their career")
- Competitor behavior ("Mac ads talk features; Apple talks feelings")
- Things you've actually observed or experienced
- Lines you've saved and loved

### Step 2 — Establish Point A and Point B

From John Henley: "The current attitude of the consumer is the starting point and the desired attitude is the finish line. You can't start a race in the middle."

Before writing a word, name:
- Point A: What does the consumer believe/feel right now?
- Point B: Where do I want them to be after reading this?

Your job is to string the wire between two telephone poles.

### Step 3 — Find the Conflict (Luke Sullivan Method)

Draw a line down the middle of the page. On one side, write what you have. On the other side, write the obvious conflict or opposite.

Three types of conflict/enemy:
- **A** — Different approaches (two ways of solving the same problem)
- **B** — Different beliefs (I believe X, you believe Y)
- **C** — Competitors (Apple vs. PC; Corvette vs. Volvo)

The conflict IS the story. Without it you have a statement. With it you have an argument. Copywriting is arguing — the conclusion is to buy your product.

Example: Seed = "1% to 2% doubles your results." Conflict = spending twice as much on ads. That conflict becomes the structure: "Throw money and pray" vs. "Learn copywriting."

### Step 4 — Iterate Without Judging

Spitballing phase. Just write. Don't evaluate yet.

The dirty water / clean water principle: The first ideas out of the tap are always muddy. You can't get to the clean water without flowing the tap. Write bad options: "Go fool Zuckerberg," "Spaghetti at War," "Fro Money and Prey." Some of these become the answer. Most are just clearing the pipe.

### Step 5 — Design in the Medium

Copy and design are the same thing. You can't separate them.

**Write in the medium where the work will be read:**
- Newsletter → write directly in ConvertKit (not Google Docs)
- Ad → design directly in Figma
- Billboard → place your ad among other billboards in Photoshop, not alone

Never write copy in isolation. You're writing for a specific context — tourist glancing at a shop sign, person on the tube, someone scrolling past seven other ads. Test the copy in that context.

Ogilvy's rule: if you take out a billboard ad, don't view it alone in Photoshop. Put it in an image with seven ads around it and people walking by. Does it still grab attention?

### Step 6 — Solve Problems One at a Time

Every fix creates a new problem. That's the job.

Harry's ad example:
- Fixed: too long → shortened the checklist line
- Created: parallelism broken ("corporate way" vs. "learn to write" don't match)
- Fixed: renamed to "copywriting way"
- Created: no symmetry in the headers
- Fixed: renamed again to "throw money and pray"
- Created: need matching visual

You're not going in circles. You're ascending a spiral. Each fix brings you closer to the version where nothing can be added or removed.

### Step 7 — Get Feedback with Multiple Versions

Don't show one version and ask "is this good?" Show three versions.

When you show one version, you get: "I like it" or "I don't like it." Unhelpful.

When you show three versions, you get: "That sentence from version 2, in the structure of version 3." Now you have something to work with.

### Step 8 — Apply the Final Polish

After the copy is locked, look for rhythm, parallelism, and visual emphasis.

Raise twice the cash / hire twice the staff / spend twice as much on ads → the repetition of "twice" creates rhythm. Pull "twice as fast" out of the body and bold it in the header. Add "that's twice as many" at the end for anyone who missed the math.

Every element should serve the argument.

## Standards as the Process

> "Your standards are your work."

9 out of 10 ideas don't make it out. Not because you lack ideas — because you have standards high enough to reject 9 of them.

The quality test: take a walk around the garden. Come back. If you can't take anything away from the copy — that's it. If you read it and one line flows to the next and you can't remove a word, you've arrived.

> "Worn by supermodels in London and dads in Ohio." You can't add a word to that. You can't take one away. It just is.

The temptation is to stop when you like it. The standard is to stop when you can't improve it.

## Collect Excellence

Your references are your raw material. Surround yourself with examples of excellent work — ads, headlines, writing, album covers, book titles, speech openings. The best copywriters can recall dozens of great examples because they've been building a mental museum for years.

When you write an ad, you're consciously or unconsciously referencing things you've admired. Harry built his ad off two inspirations: a tweet (the copy seed) and a Volkswagen ad layout (the visual structure). Having those in his mental library made the work possible.

## Creative Conditions

**Write in the medium.** Never outside it.

**Silence before writing.** Waking up on TikTok makes the day drudge. Waking up in silence — no phone, no earphones on a walk — gives you a better shot. The clearest thoughts come before the input starts.

**Retreats (optional).** Harry and Cultural Tutor (CT) do writing retreats where they don't talk until dinner. "When we sit, we don't talk till 5 or 6 when we have dinner and there's an intensity to it. God, I'm doing something here."

## What to Steal / Application

- Always identify your seed *before* you start writing. What true thing have you observed that you can build from?
- Name Point A and Point B before writing a single word of copy.
- Use the line-down-the-middle method to find your conflict. The conflict is the structure.
- Write in the medium — no more writing ads in Word docs.
- Generate 4-5 versions of every important line during editing. Show the versions to get real feedback.
- Don't evaluate during spitballing. Let the bad ideas out to get to the good ones.
- The finished piece should look inevitable. The 25 rewrites are invisible to the reader — that's the point.
