# Concept: Beverage Retail Velocity

## Summary / Definition
Beverage is a uniquely high-velocity retail category — 86% of people in the US buy a cold carbonated beverage on any given day, and almost none of them buy it online. This velocity dynamic makes beverage CPG fundamentally different from most other categories. Understanding the specific mechanics of how beverage moves in retail determines everything: when to go in, how to win, how to scale, and why some brands can reach $100M/month in revenue.

## Why It Matters
Most CPG founders learn retail mechanics the hard way. Beverage has a distinct velocity profile that creates enormous upside but also massive execution risk. Going into retail too early (without supply chain readiness) or misjudging the velocity (ordering too little or too much) can end a brand. Getting it right is how brands go from $10M to $1B.

## How It Works / The Mechanism

### The 86% Statistic
86% of people in the US will buy a cold carbonated beverage on a given day. Almost none of them are buying it online. The implication:
- The beverage market is primarily a physical retail game — not a DTC or e-commerce game
- Your product must be on-shelf in the places people already go to buy drinks
- D2C and subscription is a complementary channel, not the primary revenue driver for scale

This is why Ross Mackay's strategy for Cadence deliberately mirrors the David Protein Bar model: skip the "natural retail" sequence (farmers market → Erewhon → Sprouts → Whole Foods) and go directly to the mass retail channels where beverages actually move (Target, Walmart, CVS, Walgreens).

### The Core Metrics

**Units Per Store Per Week (USPW)**
The primary performance metric for retail success. Distribution count ("we're in 10,000 doors") is meaningless without velocity. The question is: how many units are selling in each store, each week?

- Typical beverage velocity at mass retail: 10–20 units per store per week for a performing SKU
- At 10 USPW across 3,000 stores with 3 flavors: that's a $20M+ annual run rate from a single retail account

**Dollars Per Store Per Week**
Retailers care about this number — how many dollars is your product generating per store per week? This is how you justify shelf space and win expansion.

### The 30-Day Retail Clock
In retail, you win or lose in 30-day windows. After 30 days, the data comes back. If your velocity is below expectations:
- You drop price
- You add promotion (two-for-four, etc.)
- You add end-cap or additional placement
- You risk being de-listed if velocity doesn't recover

**The trial-building loop:**
1. Enter retail with fair pricing and initial promotion
2. Promotion drives trial (two-for-one, temporary price reduction)
3. Brand often pays for the promotional gap (if everyday price is $2.50 and promo is 2 for $4, brand covers that $1 discount)
4. After promotion ends, base velocity jumps because trial-to-repeat kicks in
5. Repeat customers at EDLP (everyday low price) become the stable base
6. That stable base grows with each promotional cycle

### EDLP (Everyday Low Price) Strategy
Once trial has been built through promotion, the goal is a stable EDLP that gives you a sustainable velocity base. The cycle:
- Promotional price drives trial → velocity jumps during promo
- Returns to EDLP when promotion ends
- If the product is good, some portion of trial converts to repeat
- EDLP base velocity grows over time as the repeat base compounds

### The Natural vs. Conventional Retail Shift
Pre-COVID, the standard CPG launch sequence was:
Farmers market → Erewhon/Sprouts → Whole Foods/Bristol Farms → Regional grocery → FDM national

COVID changed this: consumers shifted to convenience-first purchasing. They order on Instacart or Amazon Prime and want product in 20 minutes. Erewhon vs. Walmart matters less when the purchase is happening through a delivery app.

The implication: going to mass retail (Walmart, Target, CVS) faster than traditional CPG sequencing would suggest is now a viable and arguably superior path — especially for brands with strong D2C communities they can redirect into retail.

### Distributor vs. Direct: The Beverage Decision

**Direct Distribution**
- You ship direct to each retail account
- Higher margin — no distributor cut (15–25%)
- Greater supply chain complexity (more trucks, more warehouses, more logistics burden)
- Better margin control allows lower pricing or higher profit

**Distributor Model**
- A third party (UNFI, KEHE, DSD networks like Dr. Pepper distributors) takes your product and delivers it to retail accounts
- Margin hit: 15–25%
- Simpler logistics — they consolidate and deliver
- Unlock additional retail accounts you haven't won directly
- But: distributors are "box movers," not salespeople. They will not sell your brand. They will move product that's already been sold.

**Brokers (different from distributors)**
Brokers are sales agents who represent your brand to retail buyers. They work on commission. Critical point: brokers have multiple clients — their "favorite child" gets the energy. Until you've proven velocity, a broker won't fight for you. The lesson: win your anchor retail customer yourself first, then use the broker to manage and expand.

**Ross's rule:** Build your own sales team. The founder should be in the retail pitch until the brand is proven. "No one's calling" if you're relying on brokers to do the work.

### Retailer Margin Requirements
Standard retail economics for beverage:
- Retailer takes 45–50% margin
- Sell-in price = RRP minus 50%
- Example: $2.79 Target RRP → ~$1.40 sell-in price
- After distribution cost and shipping, remaining margin is what you work with
- Trade spend on top of this: 20–30% of revenue for promotional activation
- Gross margin target to survive: 50%+ before trade spend

### The Scale Math
Why beverage brands can reach $100M/month:
- 3,000 doors × 3 flavors × 15 units/store/week × $2.79 = ~$112M/year from one retail account
- With multiple retail accounts (Walmart + Target + CVS + Walgreens) and national distribution, the math compounds fast
- This is a category-specific velocity advantage that most other CPG segments don't have

### When to Enter Retail
Two questions to answer before going in:
1. **Do you have the supply chain economics to make money at wholesale?** Can you sell at RRP minus 50%, cover distribution, and still operate the business? At minimum, break even. If not, don't enter retail — you'll lose money at scale.
2. **Can you supply the demand if it hits?** Being sold out at Walmart for two weeks means Walmart puts Celsius on your shelf. They are not loyal. Demand planning must be in place before entering major retail.

The mistake to avoid: entering retail before supply chain is ready. Sold out = lost shelf space = delisting.

## What to Steal / Application

**For beverage founders:**
- The 86% stat is your market size argument — almost everyone buys beverages daily, almost no one buys them online
- Unit per store per week is the only metric that matters — distribution count is vanity
- Build for mass retail from day one (printed can, retail-optimized packaging, not just D2C hero shots)
- Use D2C to build brand and prove demand, then redirect audience into retail

**For any CPG brand:**
- Understand the velocity profile of your category before choosing a distribution channel
- Not all categories behave like beverage — protein cookies don't move at 15 USPW; calibrate your expectations
- The natural-to-conventional retail sequence is no longer as sacred as it was pre-COVID
