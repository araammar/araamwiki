# Concept: Lease vs. Management Deal Portfolio

## Summary / Definition
A capital structure philosophy for hospitality operators (and, by extension, any operator building a multi-venue or multi-location business): deliberately maintain a portfolio mix of **owned leases** (full capital risk, full upside, enterprise value creation) and **management deals** (cash flow, low capital risk, no real equity ownership). Each serves a different strategic function. Relying exclusively on either model creates either a cash flow crisis or an exit value ceiling.

David Grutman codified this through hard experience at Groot Hospitality — LIV is a management deal with the Fontainebleau (cash flow machine, no capital outlay, but limited exit value), while Komodo and Poppy Steak are owned leases (capital intensive, but real IP and enterprise value at exit).

## Why It Matters
Most successful multi-venue operators intuitively run this blend — but don't think about it strategically. Making it explicit lets you:
- Know which deals are "cash flow deals" vs. "wealth creation deals"
- Avoid confusing a lucrative management fee for real equity building
- Use management deals to fund the riskier owned-lease bets
- Understand when each type is appropriate based on market and partner conditions

## How It Works / The Mechanism

### The Owned Lease
**Structure:** You sign the lease directly; you fund the build-out; you carry the operating risk.

**Economics:** You keep 100% of the upside above your lease obligation. At scale, your fixed costs (rent, HVAC, payroll) become a smaller % of revenue — your EBITDA margins expand dramatically as you grow revenue past the fixed cost threshold.

**Capital required:** $1,200–$1,500/sq ft buildout cost. A 10,000 sq ft restaurant = $12–15M before pre-opening costs.

**The prize:** Enterprise value. If the venue generates real EBITDA, you can sell it at a multiple. This is "how you gain real wealth in life" — the exit multiple on a profitable IP-owned business is incomparably larger than the sum of management fees you could ever collect.

**The trap:** Most investors who fund a single venue want IP rights at the holdco level. Grutman's learning from Komodo: the original $10M investors got pro-rata equity in all future Komodo locations. This diluted the IP value of what became a multi-market brand. The lesson: if you give IP to single-venue investors, every future location gives away value.

### The Management Deal
**Structure:** The property owner (casino, resort, hotel) retains the lease; you bring your brand, operating expertise, and programming. You take a piece of gross revenue + a piece of net profit, no capital required.

**At LIV:** Jeff Sofer (Fontainebleau) retains the real estate and capital; Grutman brings brand, celebrity relationships, and nightlife programming. LIV is one of the highest-grossing clubs in the US — the management fee is substantial — but the exit value belongs to the property owner.

**When it makes sense:**
- New markets where you want presence without capital commitment
- Partnership with a property owner who brings foot traffic (hotel guests, casino traffic) you couldn't generate alone
- Early in a brand's evolution when capital is scarce
- International expansion where local regulation or capital access is complex

**The ceiling:** Management deals generate excellent cash flow but do not create exit value the same way owned IP does. "It's a great way to make cash flow for a new restaurant operator, but it's never going to be the same as if you had the lease."

**The evolution:** As your brand matures, management deal economics improve dramatically — you can negotiate much higher management fees because the property owner needs *your* brand more than you need their building. Grutman: "Because of my name and my brands, I'm able to negotiate a pretty high management fee to where it would be the same economics as if I leased it or not."

### The Optimal Portfolio Mix
Grutman's prescription: "Have a mix of leases but also have management deals just so you know you have good cash flow coming in all the time as you make these more risky investments."

**Framework:**
- Management deals = **base layer** (predictable, low-risk cash flow that funds operations and lifestyle)
- Owned leases = **enterprise value layer** (concentrated bets on IP you control, where the exit pays you generational wealth)

The management deals provide the financial stability to take aggressive bets on owned-lease venues. Without the cash floor, a bad year at an owned-lease venue could threaten the whole operation.

**Overseas deals:** Grutman uses minimum guarantees for international management deals — a floor payment regardless of performance, appropriate when you're taking a brand into an unfamiliar market where the property owner has more location-specific risk data than you do.

## Analogues in Other Industries

This same dual-structure thinking applies beyond hospitality:

- **SaaS + services agencies:** Services revenue (management deal equivalent: steady cash, limited enterprise value) funds the SaaS product (owned IP: high-risk, high-exit-multiple)
- **Creator economy:** Brand deals and consulting = management deal equivalent; owned products/courses/platforms = owned-lease equivalent
- **CPG brands:** Private label / co-packing for others = management deal; own branded line = owned-lease equivalent
- **Franchise models:** Franchisee = management deal (operating a system you don't own); franchisor = owns the IP

## What to Steal / Application
- Before signing any deal, ask: "Am I building IP here or generating cash?" Both are valuable but they're not the same thing
- Never confuse high management fee income for equity-equivalent wealth creation
- Use lower-risk cash-generating arrangements to fund your high-risk, high-upside IP bets
- When raising money for owned IP, protect holdco-level rights — don't give investors rights over future locations or extensions
- As your brand grows, renegotiate management deal terms upward; the leverage inverts over time
