# Concept: Brand Archetypes

## Summary
A framework for deciding the role a brand plays in a consumer's world and how it should come across on social media. Choosing an archetype gives a content team a north star — a consistent style of content to execute rather than a hodgepodge of unsuccessful experiments. Brands eventually blend archetypes, but they must start by committing to one. The five archetypes described here cover the majority of brands across industries including software, fashion, food and beverage, services, resale, and manufacturing.

## How to Use This Concept
Run the archetype exercise before choosing content pillars. Start by auditing:
- Who can be on camera for the brand?
- Who can design for the brand (internal, agency, contracted)?
- What is actually happening at the company that can generate content?
- What resources can realistically be committed?

Cross-reference these resources with the archetypes below. Pick one. Build a brainstorm list of specific content ideas within that archetype. You now have a content roadmap that leadership can approve and a team can execute.

## The Five Archetypes

### The Oracle
**Core role:** The brand as the world's expert on its category.
**Best for:** Brands with access to a credible expert — a founder, staff specialist, or hireable spokesperson — who can tell intricate stories about the brand's world. Industries with depth: healthcare, legal, craftsmanship, manufacturing, finance, fragrance, design, technical SaaS.
**The question to ask:** What does our customer need or want to understand about the world we inhabit that nobody else is explaining properly?

**Content types:**
- Historical explainers — the story behind the product category, the craft, the industry
- Category comparisons — is X or Y better for Z? Which of these products is worth it?
- Why things are the way they are — explaining counterintuitive or overlooked truths
- Predictions — what happens next, based on genuine expertise

**Examples:**
- Ken Sakata (Front Office): Tells the history of workwear garments and textiles as a student-becoming-expert, building credibility before releasing his own product
- Blam Motorworks: Expert on Porsche restoration, films in the garage, explains rear steering on a 911 R, walks through active restoration projects
- Rarify (collectible furniture resale): Team members on camera explaining the history and value of pieces they sell — works with multiple people, not just one expert
- Crease Furniture founder: His personal social media runs the Oracle alongside the brand's standard account, talking trends and landscaping expert takes
- Dentist (Cut 30): Sitting in the dentist chair rating whitening products, checking if new dental trends are worth it — the formula is "is X better than Y for Z?"

**Operator tip:** The expert can be a student-perspective creator or a genuine authority. Both work. Student: "I'm learning this — here's what I discovered." Expert: "I know this category — here's what others get wrong." Wear the costume: dentist in scrubs, baker in a coat, mechanic in the garage.

### The Performer
**Core role:** The brand's feed is a show. Entertainment-first, personality-first, built to be binged and shared rather than to sell directly. The product is omnipresent but rarely the explicit topic.
**Best for:** Brands with funny, charismatic, or highly entertaining people on staff or available to hire. Works especially well for brands with humor, fashion, or lifestyle DNA.
**The question to ask:** If our brand had a show, what would it be? Would people watch it without knowing it was ours?

**Content types:**
- Recurring character skits (The Office-style workplace humor)
- Fashion or lifestyle skits where the product is always worn but rarely the topic
- Compilations of entertaining moments
- Scripted series with brand omnipresence

**Examples:**
- Dur Shoots: Fashion skits, compilations, travel content — the clothes are always on but the topic is the situation. Budgets expanded over time to include travel.
- Mohawk Chevrolet: Did a spin-off of The Office on TikTok. Car dealership as office comedy — the business is omnipresent in the setting without being the topic.
- LC Signs: Every post is a skit that leads into product promotion. Pure entertainment hook on all content.
- Cluey (as Performer): Scripted, well-produced office skits with a recurring cast of characters — all iPhone, all scripted, all narrative-driven

### The World Builder
**Core role:** The brand creates content so interesting, far-reaching, and surprising that people love the brand by association. Content goes well beyond the brand's direct product world. Short films, fictional characters, immersive intellectual curation.
**Best for:** Teams and founders willing to take creative risk. Best when the brand's own product category feels constrained for content and there's genuine creative talent available.
**The question to ask:** Can we make something creative that people will love so much that they love our brand right next to it?

**Content types:**
- Short films or fictional series — recurring cast, narrative throughline
- Immersive intellectual content — deep dives into adjacent culture, visual trends, industry phenomena
- Curation of the world the brand inhabits (not just the product itself)

**Examples:**
- Death to Stock (stock photo brand): Talks about what's happening in the visual world — packaging trends, cyber deck aesthetics, emerging art movements. The brand is contextually implied ("our stock photos are on trend") but the content is cultural.
- Cluey (as World Builder): Fictional office skits about a made-up company narrative. All iPhone. Fully scripted. The best content of anyone in their competitive space despite having no functioning product.
- Fern (fragrance): Short films about the essence of what the fragrance represents — artistic, esoteric, distinctive. High creative investment.

**Risk note:** This is the highest-effort, highest-upside archetype. When it fails, it fails invisibly (nobody cares). When it works, it builds the deepest brand affinity. Only appropriate when you've decided the standard playbook isn't working and you're willing to make a real bet.

### The Catalyst
**Core role:** Content that pushes people toward a better version of themselves. The brand is the bridge between who the audience is and who they want to be. Educational but not expert-dependent. Aspirational and motivational.
**Best for:** Brands selling transformation — fitness, wellness, finance, productivity, career growth, lifestyle. Also works for B2B brands helping clients achieve professional ambitions.
**The question to ask:** How can we be proof and guidance for how our demographic achieves what they want?

**Content types:**
- Training and process documentation
- Inspirational text-overlay content (single-shot, simple but motivational)
- Community highlights and challenges
- Curation of the world their audience wants to live in

**Examples:**
- Crease Group (manufacturing): Breaks down what makes a good manufacturer brief, what a simple product really costs, how a laser manufacturing process works, trade show highlights for their clients who can't attend. Helps the audience become more sophisticated buyers and operators.
- Artificcing: Curates content their community sends them — an audience that wants to participate in multi-way conversation
- Base Living: Lifestyle curation account with catchphrases and meme-adjacent content for people seeking a back-to-earth lifestyle. No one on camera required.

### The Helper
**Core role:** Practical, useful content from someone who's just a step ahead of the audience — not a deep expert, not a charismatic performer. A relatable guide who answers the audience's real questions. The difference from the Catalyst: the Catalyst is aspirational (here's who you could become), the Helper is practical (here's what to do today).
**Best for:** Brands where the category requires customer education, where the product is complex or unfamiliar, or where the customer has many options and needs guidance to decide. Effective for aggregators, curators, and multi-product retailers.
**The question to ask:** What are the little helpful things we can do that our customer will appreciate, and what are the relatable anecdotes they'll connect with?

**Content types:**
- Visual tutorials (carousel or video — doesn't require on-camera expert)
- Light tips and how-to content
- Story vlogs and relatable anecdotes
- Interviews and curations from within the category

**Examples:**
- Kiyoko Beauty (Korean skincare retailer): Multiple creators breaking down Korean skincare basics — "don't buy this serum, get this instead" — not from a chemist's authority but from a fellow shopper's perspective
- Hashihome: Host-life relatable content — skits and situations that anyone who hosts dinner parties will recognize. Products (ceramics) are present but not lectured about.
- Marpipe (catalog ads software): Creator on brand account posting helpful content about marketing trends and what's happening in the ad world — most of it loosely related to the product. Result: the brand is more recognized and ads perform better because viewers have ambient familiarity.

## Why Archetypes Matter for Organizations
The archetype framework translates marketing strategy into terms leadership understands. CMOs and executives already know archetype thinking from traditional brand strategy. Bringing it down to social media content makes the conversation less about "should we post more" and more about "which persona do we want to own in the market."

For a younger employee pitching a new content direction to leadership, an archetype exercise — with resource mapping and a concrete idea list at the end — creates buy-in faster than a content calendar because it answers the strategic question before the tactical one.
