# Concept: Hierarchy of Metrics (DTC)

## Summary

Taylor Holiday's four-tier pyramid showing which metrics actually matter in DTC — and in what order. The pyramid prevents fixation on vanity metrics disconnected from business value. Platform metrics (ROAS, CPM, CPC) are not decisions — they are information. The only scoreboard that matters is contribution margin.

---

## The Four Tiers

```
         ┌─────────────────────────┐
         │   TIER 1: FINANCIAL     │
         │   Contribution Margin   │
         └────────────┬────────────┘
                      │
         ┌────────────▼────────────┐
         │  TIER 2: BUSINESS       │
         │  Revenue / MER / AOV    │
         └────────────┬────────────┘
                      │
         ┌────────────▼────────────┐
         │  TIER 3: NEW CUSTOMERS  │
         │  CAC / New Cust MER     │
         └────────────┬────────────┘
                      │
         ┌────────────▼────────────┐
         │  TIER 4: PLATFORM       │
         │  ROAS / CPM / CPC       │
         └─────────────────────────┘
```

Each tier serves the one above it. Platform metrics inform Tier 3 decisions; Tier 3 decisions serve Tier 2 outcomes; Tier 2 outcomes are in service of Tier 1 health.

---

## Tier 1: Financial Metrics (The Scorecard)

**Contribution Margin (CM):**
```
Net Sales − Cost of Delivery − Ad Spend = Contribution Margin
```
- "Your business's scoreboard" — the one number showing if you're winning
- Unlike MER or ROAS, accounts for ALL variable costs
- Directly tied to "money in your pocket"
- Build daily CM targets and track daily actuals against forecast

**Why daily tracking matters:** A 5% error in contribution margin calculation can transform "profitable" campaigns into money-losers. The earlier you see the variance, the more options you have.

---

## Tier 2: Business Metrics

| Metric | What It Shows |
|---|---|
| **Order Revenue** | Total top-line — necessary but insufficient on its own |
| **Ad Spend** | Deployed capital — only meaningful relative to CM, not as a standalone |
| **Marketing Efficiency Ratio (MER)** | Total Revenue ÷ Total Marketing Spend — post-iOS 14 north star metric |
| **Average Order Value (AOV)** | Affects both CM and CAC math |

**MER vs. ROAS:** MER replaced ROAS as the primary efficiency metric for serious operators post-iOS 14 because platform-reported ROAS is attribution fiction. MER is observable: you know your total revenue and total ad spend regardless of attribution model.

---

## Tier 3: New Customer Metrics

| Metric | What It Shows |
|---|---|
| **Total New Customers** | Growth rate of the active customer file |
| **New Customer CAC** | True cost to acquire — not blended with reactivated customers |
| **New Customer MER** | Efficiency on first-purchase customers specifically |
| **Repeat Purchase Rate** | Signal of brand health and LTV trajectory |

**The trap:** Blending new customer and returning customer metrics hides what's really happening. High overall MER with low new customer count = you're squeezing existing customers, not growing.

---

## Tier 4: Platform Metrics (Information, Not Decisions)

| Metric | How to Use It |
|---|---|
| **ROAS** | Directional signal — not a target, not a success criterion |
| **CPM** | Competitive landscape indicator for a given audience |
| **CPC** | Creative efficiency signal — useful for diagnosing creative problems |
| **CTR** | Hook effectiveness at the creative level |

**The rule:** Never make a business decision based solely on a Tier 4 metric. Platform attribution is imperfect and self-serving. A 4.0 ROAS with a negative contribution margin is failure, not success.

---

## The MER Trap

The common mistake: treating MER as a fixed target. "We need to maintain 3.5 MER."

The problem: defending a fixed MER often means sacrificing new customer acquisition. You squeeze maximum revenue from existing customers (high MER, low new customers) and the active customer file shrinks. This is the [[shrinking-sponge-problem]] in real time.

**The right approach:** Accept temporary efficiency decreases to acquire new customers for long-term profitability. MER is a guardrail, not a target.

---

## What to Steal

Build a single dashboard with all four tiers visible simultaneously. The goal is to be able to trace a platform-level anomaly ("ROAS dropped this week") all the way up to its business impact ("contribution margin is down 8% vs. forecast"). Without the hierarchy, every metric problem feels equally urgent. With it, you can triage.

## DTC Payback Period Benchmarks

The payback period (time to recoup CAC from subscription or repeat revenue) is the signal that determines how aggressively you can spend. Danny Yeung's (I Am Ath) benchmarks based on running a subscription DTC brand to $100M+ ARR:

| Payback Period | Grade | Implication |
|---|---|---|
| Under 4 months | World-class | You can likely push spend harder — you're leaving scale on the table |
| 4–6 months | Excellent | Strong position; explore cautious spend increases |
| 6–12 months | Good / C-minus | Viable but margin of error is thin |
| 12+ months | Danger zone | Every inefficiency in CAC compounds before you recoup |

**Key insight:** If you're at under-4-month payback, you're "almost too efficient" — you could absorb a higher CAC (longer payback) and still be highly profitable, meaning you're under-spending relative to your unit economics ceiling.

Source: Danny Yeung, I Am Ath ($0 → $100M ARR in 11 months), [[xE_algbPrwQ-danny-yeung-open-residency]]
