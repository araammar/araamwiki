# Concept: Price Pack Architecture

## Summary / Definition
The strategic design of different product sizes, serving counts, and price points for different retail channels — ensuring each channel's version of the product serves that specific shopper without cannibalizing sales in other channels.

Dom Iacovone has a dedicated ex-P&G executive whose entire job is price pack architecture and anti-cannibalization across RAW/Bum's retail footprint.

## Why It Matters
Without deliberate price pack architecture:
- Your Costco shopper drives to Walmart to save money (Costco loses velocity, Walmart wins on lower margin)
- Your specialty shopper discovers the same product at Walmart for half the price (Vitamin Shoppe loses and your brand loses premium positioning)
- You create internal margin cannibalism — the same consumer shifting channels hurts total blended margin

Dom: *"A Walmart shopper is different than a Target shopper. A Costco shopper is different than a Sam's shopper. A Vitamin Shoppe shopper is different than a Walmart shopper."*

## How It Works / The Mechanism

**The framework:**
Each retail channel gets a version of the product calibrated to:
- **The shopper's willingness to pay** at that channel
- **The serving count / size** appropriate for that shopping occasion
- **The retailer's margin requirements** (club ~15%, specialty ~40–50%)
- **The price point on shelf** that doesn't cannibalize other channels

**Channel profiles:**

| Channel | Shopper Profile | Typical Margin | Price Point |
|---------|----------------|---------------|-------------|
| Vitamin Shoppe / GNC | Enthusiast, high-intent | 40–50% | Premium |
| Target | Mainstream, lifestyle | 35–45% | Mid-premium |
| Walmart | Value-conscious, mass | 35–45% | Mid-mass |
| Publix / HEB | Grocery shopper | 35–45% | Mid |
| Sam's Club | Bulk buyer, member | ~15% | Lower/unit |
| Costco | Same as Sam's | ~15% | Lower/unit |

**Serving sizes as the lever:**
Rather than just changing the price, brands change serving sizes. A 30-serving tub at Vitamin Shoppe vs. a 20-serving version at Walmart maintains category exclusivity and prevents direct price comparison.

**The Walmart buyer lesson:**
Dom's biggest early retail mistake: a Walmart buyer told them what SKUs to create for the Walmart entrance. They complied. The products failed because they weren't aligned to RAW's brand vision — they were built to hit a price point the buyer wanted, not a product the RAW audience would buy.

The lesson: **buyers don't know what they don't know.** Your brand DNA must be preserved even when entering mass retail. The products that work in your specialty channels are usually the ones that work in mass if packaged correctly for that shelf.

**The dedicated role:**
At scale, Dom has a dedicated person managing:
- Gross-to-net waterfall (trade spend by channel)
- Anti-cannibalization between channels
- Serving sizes and MSRP by retailer
- Channel P&L glide path

This person's analysis revealed that the blended gross-to-net across all channels was above 10% — meaning on $100M gross revenue, $10M was being lost before the P&L even started. (See [[gross-to-net-waterfall]].)

## What to Steal / Application
- **Map your channels before you're in them.** Design the product version for each channel before signing contracts.
- **Serve size differentiation > pure price differentiation.** Changes the product experience and makes price comparison harder.
- **Never let a retailer buyer dictate your SKU creation.** They don't know your brand. You do. Bring your best product, packaged for their shelf environment.
- **Build a P&L by retailer.** This is the only way to see which channels are contributing and which are leaking.
- **Once you're in multiple channels, hire for this function.** An ex-P&G commercial strategy officer is worth their salary many times over.
