# Concept: How to Develop Taste

## Definition
Taste is the cultivated ability to evaluate, critique, and make decisions about quality across creative and aesthetic domains — design, music, fashion, literature, food, objects. Contrary to common belief, taste is not innate or class-inherited; it is learned, structured, and progressively developed through deliberate exposure, critical engagement, and public defense of opinions. It is also a lifelong practice, not a destination.

## Why It Matters in Marketing
In a world where production quality has been democratized and every brand can access good-looking visuals and competent copy, taste is the differentiator that cannot be templated. Technology firms now actively recruit for it. Brands with taste convert better and command higher prices — not because of their product, but because of how they present it, curate it, and position it in culture. For marketers and creatives, taste is increasingly the skill gap that separates commodity work from premium positioning.

## How It Works

### The Foundation: Two Levels
Developing taste begins at two interconnected levels:
1. **Human perception fundamentals** — The near-universal visual and sensory principles that humans respond to, whether consciously or not.
2. **Historical and cultural knowledge** — Understanding what has already been deemed excellent by broad human consensus across time.

These two levels interact: knowing the rules helps you see when and why someone is breaking them to great effect.

### Level 1: Learning the Immutable Principles
Every creative domain has a set of foundational principles that govern what "looks right" or "feels good" even to untrained observers:
- **Visual design**: Rule of thirds, grid systems, typographic hierarchy, color theory. Work that abides by these principles naturally resonates; work that violates them without intention looks "off."
- **Audio**: Production quality, layering, dynamics, emotional arc.
- **Experience**: Rhythm, contrast, pacing, surprise.

The starting resource: a history of graphic design textbook (e.g., Meggs' *History of Graphic Design*) covers every major visual period — Art Nouveau, Art Deco, Bauhaus, Swiss Design, Memphis Movement, Modernism. A single pass through this gives you the vocabulary to identify what you are looking at and why it works.

### Level 2: Building Historical Knowledge Across Domains
Taste requires a canon — not because the canon is infallible, but because it provides a reference framework. For four key domains:

- **Design**: Study major movements from the 20th century forward. Know what Bauhaus was, what Swiss poster design achieved, what the Memphis movement was reacting against. You develop a vocabulary to describe and judge what you see.
- **Literature**: Engage with classic texts not as a duty but as a study. Why do people consider this a classic? What does it do that ordinary writing doesn't?
- **Music**: Explore origination points — the earliest house music, the blues that gave rise to rock, jazz as the foundation of modern harmony. Context changes how you hear everything downstream.
- **Fashion**: Know what McQueen did in a given category before buying from Zara. Understanding historical reference makes contemporary choices legible.

**Practical prescription**: Once a week, engage with one thing from one of these categories. Not to consume — to analyze.

### The Critique Framework: Composition, Effectivity, Vibes
Once you have foundational knowledge, you need a framework for evaluating anything in real time. Three lenses:

1. **Composition** — How is it put together? Is it well-made? Does the material feel right? Is the typography balanced? Is the music well-produced with layers that create depth? This is the technical execution layer.

2. **Effectivity** — Does it actually work? Does the quarter-zip look good when worn? Does the music make people want to dance or feel something? Does the ad generate clicks? Composition and effectivity can diverge: a thing can be technically masterful and still miss.

3. **Vibes** — The intangible. Does it have something special? Is it unique? Some things are well-composed and effective but missing the last layer — there's no "it." The vibes meter identifies the presence or absence of that ineffable quality.

These three lenses together let you say: "The composition is strong, effectivity is there, but the vibes are flat — and here's why." Or: "The vibes are compelling but the execution is amateur." This is how taste becomes articulable rather than just felt.

### Living Your Taste: Decision-Making as Practice
Knowing about taste and embodying taste are different things. Taste becomes real in decisions:
- When buying clothes: Do you research the category (what did McQueen do here? what makes a garment last?) or just buy the familiar brand?
- When furnishing a space: Do you make considered choices about what belongs, or fill space with convenient purchases?
- When consuming media: Do you choose deliberately (a classic album, a significant book) or default to whatever the algorithm serves?

**The thrift store test**: The best exercise for developing taste is assembling a complete, genuinely good outfit from a thrift store. No brand guidance, no price signal — only your own compositional sense. It forces you to apply the critique framework without the crutch of a label or recommendation. The same principle applies to furnishing a home with secondhand pieces.

### Defending Your Opinions: The Social Layer
The difference between knowing about taste and having taste is the ability to articulate and defend it in conversation. Taste becomes sharpened through:
- Putting opinions out publicly — written, spoken, as content — and having them tested by other perspectives.
- Being willing to say "I think this album is objectively weaker than X because of Y and Z" and then engaging with disagreement.
- Working in environments where aesthetic decisions are discussed constantly (fashion, architecture, food) accelerates taste development faster than any self-study.

When people begin seeking your opinion on aesthetics — "what knife should I buy," "what album is worth listening to," "what do you think of this design" — you know taste has become externally visible. This is the signal, not a follower count or a title.

### Taste as a Progression
The development arc:
1. **Exposure and foundation building** — Consume deliberately from the canon. Build the reference library.
2. **Critique application** — Apply the composition/effectivity/vibes framework to everything you encounter.
3. **Decision living** — Let taste drive purchasing, curation, and consumption choices.
4. **Public defense** — Share opinions and test them against others. This sharpens and clarifies.
5. **Experience and travel** — Spending time in different cultural contexts (other countries, subcultures, industries) reshapes taste in ways that books alone cannot.

Starting age is irrelevant. The practice compounds over a lifetime. The goal is not to arrive — it is to be in continuous, engaged movement.

## Key Takeaways
- Taste is entirely learnable. It is not innate, not class-exclusive, and not the exclusive province of trained designers.
- Start with foundational principles (grid systems, color theory, design history), then build a personal canon across design, literature, music, and fashion.
- Use the composition/effectivity/vibes framework to make your aesthetic judgments specific and articulable.
- Taste is only real when it drives decisions. The thrift store exercise is a forcing function.
- Public defense of opinions is the accelerant. Sharing your take — and having it challenged — is how taste actually sharpens.
- Depth of engagement with a subculture (K-pop, menswear, fragrance) generates taste faster than broad shallow consumption.
