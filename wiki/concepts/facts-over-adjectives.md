# Concept: Facts Over Adjectives
tags: [copywriting, writing, persuasion, specificity, precision, concrete-language]

## Summary / Definition

Harry Dry's philosophy: a fact guarantees you say something. Most people open their mouths (or keyboards) and say nothing — what he calls "wallpaper, word-shaped air, balloon smoke." Facts ground your writing in reality and give you a starting point for a story.

This isn't about dry data. It's about using what's verifiably true as the *foundation* before building anything else on top.

## Why It Matters

The most common writing failure is vagueness. Adjectives feel like they're saying something — "innovative," "quality," "passionate," "seamless" — but they convey nothing. They're interchangeable across any brand in any category. Anyone can claim them.

A fact is different. It's specific. It belongs to one moment, one product, one experience. It cannot be claimed by anyone who didn't observe it.

More importantly: a fact can be turned into a story. An adjective cannot.

## How It Works / The Mechanism

### The Core Rule: If in Doubt, Give Me a Fact

> "A fact guarantees that you say something. Most people when they open their mouths, they say nothing — wallpaper, word-shaped air, balloon smoke. A fact is: you're giving me something. This is true."

Facts don't have to be numerical. The Heinz ketchup story is a fact: some restaurants take factory ketchup and pour it into a Heinz bottle. From that fact, you can write an ad: "Even when it's not Heinz, it's Heinz." That's not a number — it's an observable truth about behavior.

### Start With a Fact, Then Build

You don't have to end with the fact. Start there.

The flight example: "The flight's only 6 hours. Too short to sleep. Too long to be easy." That's a fact — and inside it is a complete story about why the east coast → London flight is miserable. The fact grounds the observation; then the story builds.

Harry's marketing examples newsletter opener: he looks out the window. "Live from London. Weather update: trees shuttering. Majestic hail." He didn't invent it — he saw it. You can't get that from Twitter. It happened, and that fact gave him a newsletter intro.

### Precision as Trust Signal

Imprecision sounds like every other piece of content. Precision sounds like someone who actually knows.

Bad sports commentary: "Tiger Woods just didn't want it bad enough today."
Good sports commentary: "Tiger Woods usually averages 11 fairways per round. Today he averaged 7. He made up for that with an extra 30% greens in regulation and was three times more likely to make a one-putt."

Once rooted in fact, everything that follows carries more weight. The analysis feels earned. The conclusion feels credible.

The same principle applies to marketing commentary, copy, cold emails, and social content. If your argument starts with a specific, verifiable fact, the reader can't dismiss it as opinion.

### "Don't Say Inequality, Show Me the Graph"

This is the falsifiability test applied to writing:

- Don't say "this is unequal." Show the graph. Point at the number.
- Don't say "our product is better." Cite what's better and by how much.
- Don't say "customers love this." Quote one specific customer, in their specific words, about one specific use.

The reader can't argue with a graph. They can dismiss an adjective in one second.

### Facts as Copy Seeds

Some of the best copy in history is just a fact with excellent placement:

**Ogilvy's Rolls-Royce headline (1958):**
> "At 60 mph, the loudest noise coming from the Rolls-Royce is the sound of an electric clock."

Ogilvy didn't invent this. He pulled it from a motor magazine. The journalist had sat in the car, driven it at 60 mph, and noticed the clock. Ogilvy had the taste to recognize it as the headline. The fact increased Rolls-Royce sales by 50%.

**Harry's copywriting course ad:**
> "Marketers, you'll spend 22,000 hours of your career writing. Spend two learning how to do it well."

The seed was a calculation: 4 hours/day × 300 days × 30-year career = ~22,000 hours. The fact made the scale undeniable. He deliberately chose 22,000 (instead of the exact 18,000 his calculation produced) for the parallelism with "spend two." The fact drove the copy; the parallelism made it memorable.

**Tesla Model S (Ad Professor):**
> "It takes 3.1 seconds to read this ad. The same time it takes a Model S to go from 0 to 60."

The 3.1 seconds is precise, verifiable, and breaks the fourth wall. The juxtaposition of reading time and acceleration time does all the persuasion.

## What to Steal / Application

- Before writing any piece of copy, ask: what's the most interesting *true* thing about this product/brand/story? Start there.
- Replace every adjective ("quality," "innovative," "seamless") with the specific fact that would make someone believe the adjective is true.
- If you're writing about a problem, find the number that proves it ("marketers spend 22,000 hours writing").
- If you're describing an experience, find the specific observable detail that carries the experience ("the loudest noise is an electric clock").
- Use facts as openers — they earn the right to tell a story.
- When giving feedback or commentary, root your observations in data before interpretation. Not "they didn't want it enough" — "they hit 7 of 11 fairways."
