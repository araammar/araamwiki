# Concept: Single-SKU Depth Before Breadth

## Summary / Definition
A product development discipline for early-stage CPG brands: concentrate all capital, production volume, and organizational energy on a single SKU until it reaches a defined revenue threshold (typically $10M) before investing in the next SKU. The principle rejects the intuition that more products = more revenue, and argues instead that premature SKU proliferation dilutes the cost structure, splits operational focus, and sacrifices compounding economics.

Ross Mackay: *"I'm obsessed to run businesses that can build a several hundred million dollar business with one SKU. Put all your wood behind one arrow."*

## Why It Matters
Founders instinctively want to expand. New flavors, new formats, new categories feel like growth. In practice, premature expansion:
- Spreads manufacturing volume across multiple suppliers, reducing purchasing power
- Increases supply chain complexity (different manufacturers, different lead times, different terms)
- Forces internal attention onto too many things simultaneously
- Creates cash flow risk (each new SKU requires inventory before it generates revenue)
- Can confuse the brand signal before the core product is established

The compounding logic runs in reverse too: every unit of capital and attention you put into SKU #1 drives down the cost of goods, improves manufacturing relationships, and builds the case for retail buyers. Splitting that capital across SKUs 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 prevents any of them from reaching critical mass.

## How It Works / The Mechanism

### The Economic Case

When all capital flows to one SKU and one manufacturer:
- Volume increases → cost of goods decreases (bulk purchasing, scale economies)
- Manufacturer relationship deepens → better terms, priority treatment, co-investment in innovation
- Supply chain efficiency improves → lead times shorten, forecasting accuracy increases
- Product quality compounds → more iterations, more learning, stronger formulation over time

When capital is split across multiple SKUs and multiple manufacturers:
- Each SKU orders at smaller volumes → higher unit costs
- Relationships with multiple manufacturers → more management overhead, worse terms at each
- Cash tied up in multiple inventory positions → liquidity strain
- Less learning per SKU → slower product improvement

### The $10M Threshold Rule
Don't launch a new SKU until the existing core SKU reaches $10M in revenue. The threshold is category-dependent, but the principle is universal: the primary SKU must have proven itself — both in consumer demand and in operational efficiency — before bandwidth is diverted to the next product.

Why $10M? At that level:
- The product has retail proof (buyers have given it shelf space and it's turning)
- Manufacturing relationships are established and volume-based pricing is accessible
- The supply chain is understood and repeatable
- The brand has enough credibility to earn retailer interest in a second SKU

Below $10M, "earning the right" to expand horizontally hasn't happened yet. Retailers who say "do you have other flavors?" are asking as a future option, not as an immediate requirement.

### The 80/20 Brand-Building SKU
There is one exception to the rule: SKUs that serve a brand-building function even with small commercial upside.

The logic: sometimes a product is small in revenue but essential for brand credibility with the core ICP. Cadence's example — a carbohydrate powder for endurance athletes:
- Small category, small commercial ceiling
- But: it reinforces the brand's positioning as a serious performance nutrition company
- It keeps the brand "grounded in performance" when the hero can product is pushing into mass retail

This is an 80/20 decision: 80% of focus on the commercial SKU, 20% on the credibility/ICP-cementing innovation that keeps the brand anchored.

**The test for a brand-building SKU:** Does this product, even if it never exceeds $1M in revenue, strengthen the brand's core positioning in a way that makes the hero product more credible? If yes, it may be worth building. If it's just a distraction based on curiosity or opportunity, it's not.

### The Anchor Customer Underwriting Gate
No new SKU gets approved until there is a committed retail anchor customer for it. This prevents the "build it and hope they come" inventory trap.

The process:
1. Identify the new SKU concept and validate the formulation
2. Take it to key retail buyers as a future offering
3. Only when a buyer says "yes, we will launch this in [month]" do you press go with the manufacturer
4. Production begins with a committed purchase order in hand

This transforms product launches from inventory bets into demand-pull manufacturing — dramatically reducing cash flow risk.

Without this gate, the early-stage company ends up sitting on inventory of multiple SKUs, betting on D2C velocity that may not materialize. Inventory is cash. Cash runs the business.

### Flavor Expansion as Phase 2 SKU Strategy
For beverage and food brands, flavor variants are a special case. Retailers often want multiple options even at launch ("do you have other flavors?"). The practical approach:
- Start with one SKU, one flavor
- Earn retailer interest in flavor variety after demonstrating sell-through on the core
- Use retailer demand as the trigger for flavor expansion — not internal enthusiasm
- Keep flavor variants at the same manufacturer to maintain volume consolidation

**Cadence's application:** Started with citrus flavor. Once the retailer validated that flavor and asked about alternatives, earned the right to add melon berry and others. Three flavors going into Walmart/Target — but each anchored by prior velocity proof.

### Production Consolidation Principle
When secondary SKUs are eventually added, keep them at the same manufacturer wherever possible. Having multiple manufacturers introduces:
- Different quality standards to manage
- Different lead times to synchronize
- Different relationships to maintain
- Different pricing negotiations

The tradeoff: some categories (bars vs. beverages, powders vs. RTD) genuinely require different manufacturers because the production process is incompatible. But where there is flexibility, consolidating manufacturing preserves the volume advantages of focusing.

## What to Steal / Application

**For any CPG founder:** Before launching SKU #2, ask: has SKU #1 reached $10M? Has it earned retailer interest in expansion? If not, the next SKU is almost certainly a distraction.

**For beverage brands specifically:** The RTD can is likely to be 90% of your business. Every other format (sachet, gel, bar) feeds brand credibility and high-margin subscription — but don't let them consume manufacturing bandwidth that should go to the hero can.

**For retail-bound brands:** Use retailer conversations as your SKU expansion gate. If a buyer asks "what else do you have?", that's the signal to develop the answer. If no buyer has asked, no new SKU is needed yet.
