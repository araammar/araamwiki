# Concept: IP Ownership Discipline

## Summary / Definition
The principle that **real wealth in business is built at the exit, not through annual earnings** — and that exits are only valuable when you own the intellectual property (the brand, the concept, the name, the system). Every deal structure, partnership, and investment decision should be evaluated through the lens of: "Am I retaining IP ownership here, and if so, on what terms?" If someone else controls the IP at the exit, you may have built something valuable for them but not yourself.

Codified by David Grutman through the Komodo mistake: early investors in the $10M raise received pro-rata equity in all future Komodo locations — not just a return on the original venue. The lesson: those investors own a percentage of every Komodo opened anywhere in the world, because the deal was structured at the holdco (IP) level. That cost Grutman equity in what became a multi-market brand worth far more than the original investment.

## Why It Matters
Most early operators think at the **single-venue or single-project level** — "Can I make this work?" The IP ownership mindset shifts the question to: "If this becomes a multi-unit, multi-market brand, who benefits?"

The difference in outcomes between "operational success with poor IP terms" and "operational success with full IP ownership" can be a 10x–100x wealth difference at exit.

## How It Works / The Mechanism

### The IP Ownership Spectrum
**Full IP ownership (holdco level):** You own the brand, the name, the concept, and all future applications. A $10M revenue venue with full IP ownership is worth the exit multiple on the entire brand — including every future location. This is Grutman's position on Poppy Steak and Komodo (minus the early dilution).

**Partial IP / revenue share:** Investors hold a percentage of the IP at the holdco, meaning they participate in every location ever opened under that brand. This is what happened with Komodo's original investors. They deserve it — they took the risk — but the cost to Grutman was equity in a global multi-market brand, not just a single-venue return.

**No IP / management deal:** You operate under someone else's property and brand framework. Zero exit value — you're a service provider. Maximum cash flow, minimum wealth creation. LIV nightclub is the example.

### The Decision Framework
Before any deal, Grutman applies this filter:
1. "Am I going to be able to grow this?" — Is there multi-location potential?
2. "Should my partner be on the IP, or is this just an investment deal?" — Does this partner add enough value (operational, capital, network) to deserve holdco-level equity, or are they purely financial?
3. "If they're on the IP, what's the right mechanism?" — IP fee, first right of refusal to invest in new locations, pro-rata equity, or a fixed percentage?

**The hard line:** When approached for partnerships, Grutman asks directly: "Am I going to be part of that IP?" If the answer is no — "We just want you to be the Miami operator" — he declines. Because the money you make on that particular year or deal is not the wealth-building event. The exit is.

### The Corollary: Always Build with Exit in Mind
This principle reshapes how you think about every creative and business decision:

- Building something that could be licensed, franchised, or sold → IP matters
- Building an operator-dependent business that can't survive without you → IP is worthless even if you own it
- Giving equity to investors at the holdco level when they're funding a single venue → You've accidentally given them a position in every future market you enter

### IP Beyond Hospitality
The same principle applies across industries:
- An agency that builds a proprietary methodology, tool, or platform owns IP; pure service delivery does not
- A creator who builds a course, software tool, or brand owns IP; platform revenue does not
- A CPG brand owner controls IP; a contract manufacturer or retailer partnership does not
- Any business that could be replicated in a new market or sold to a strategic buyer has IP value

## What to Steal / Application
- Every deal: ask "who owns the IP here and what does that mean at the exit?"
- When raising capital for a scalable business, consider carefully whether investors receive holdco-level or single-project-level rights — the difference compounds enormously
- If a partner is only adding capital (not operational value, network, or expertise proportional to their equity stake), consider giving them project-level returns rather than brand-level ownership
- Build businesses that could be sold. Not every business needs an exit, but if you never think about exit structure, you're leaving wealth on the table
- "Management deal" thinking vs. "lease thinking" maps onto every business model — identify which mode you're in and make sure it's deliberate

## Named Lesson: The Komodo Mistake
Grutman explicitly calls this out: "In the beginning, I didn't really know what IP meant and I only looked at things as a single venue and not multiple venues." The $10M investors at Komodo took a risk — it was their first restaurant, unproven. They deserved a return. But the deal structure gave them pro-rata equity at the holdco level, meaning they participate in every Komodo opened in Vegas, Dallas, or anywhere else. That's a reasonable outcome for people who funded a brand's origins, but it represents an accidental transfer of multi-market wealth.

The evolution: Grutman now structures deals so that investors in a single project can reinvest in future locations (first right of refusal) but don't automatically own IP across the entire brand system.
