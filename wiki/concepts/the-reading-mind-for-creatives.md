# Concept: The Reading Mind for Creatives

## Definition

The reading mind for creatives is not simply a habit of reading. It is a specific approach to intellectual development — one that prioritizes building a mental model library over consuming information, that chooses books for the quality of the lens they install rather than the specific facts they contain, and that treats reading across categories (history, fiction, craft, business, politics, psychology) as the only reliable way to develop a genuinely original perspective in a world where everyone's algorithm is converging. The reading mind is built against the current default, which is algorithm-curated content that produces the illusion of breadth while delivering a narrower and narrower range of reference.

---

## Why It Matters

"We all scroll these same algorithms and no matter how nuanced or interesting we think we are, we're being shown a lot of the same content. We're also listening to a lot of the same creatives. I can count on my hands how many big marketing and branding accounts there are — most of us are friends, we're in group chats, we're sharing a lot of the similar stuff." The problem of convergent reference — everyone drawing from the same pool of inspiration — is the defining creative problem of the internet era. Books are one of the few remaining tools that can break you out of the feed.

For a creative, the reading mind has a specific function: it builds the lens. It is not about being more informed than other people. It is about seeing things differently — having a genuinely independent perspective on a problem because your reference library contains things that most people's doesn't. "I tried to pick a list of things I feel really will help shape your mind for the kind of decisions you have to make in the modern world."

---

## The Learning Philosophy

### Reading to Build a Lens, Not to Collect Facts

The most important function of a book is not the information it contains — it is the perspective it installs. "The lens with which I view the world" is what reading at its best produces. The creator who reads Capslock does not come away with a set of design facts. They come away with a framework for seeing how graphical presentation shapes economic reality — and that framework changes every subsequent visual decision they make.

"I didn't bring out any of the books I'm reading right now... I'm reading things specifically to learn how to critically think, because that helps my work as a strategist." The reading program here is explicitly about method — about how to think — not about what to know. This is the distinction between information consumption and mental model building.

### Reading Across Categories as Competitive Advantage

Every creative in the same field is reading the same books — the ones recommended in the same communities, the ones assigned in the same schools, the ones reviewed in the same publications. Reading across categories is how you build a reference library that no one else in your immediate creative context has.

The book categories in this framework are not accidental:
- **Oral histories** — for temporal context: understanding how this moment is continuous with past moments, not exceptional
- **Literary fiction** — for world-building: understanding how immersive environments are created through language
- **Historical fiction** — for scale and stakes: understanding greatness, failure, and the decisions that define an era
- **Politics/journalism** — for perspective: understanding how narratives are constructed and contested
- **Business and operations** — for reality-testing: understanding the structural requirements of turning creative vision into lasting enterprise
- **Craft and design** — for technical vocabulary: understanding the specific language of the materials you work with

The argument is not that you should read all categories equally. It is that your reading diet should be diverse enough that your mental model library contains things that people in your immediate peer group don't have.

### The Reading Practice

The recommended practice is simple and sustainable: "30 minutes before sleep. I will try to be off all devices, no screens, and then get some reading time in." This produces roughly one book per month at a consistent pace, occasionally two. "I used to read a book a week when I was younger, pre-social media." The comparison is instructive — the reading rate is correlated with the amount of time not spent on screens, not with available free time.

The guidance on difficult books: "Don't let the density of the first chunks of this get you down. Literally skip through this book. Open up any section of page and you're going to find some really interesting nugget." Dense, foundational books are not meant to be read linearly — they are reference volumes you return to for specific sections, conversations, and problems.

### Why Fiction Belongs in a Creative's Library

"We are a very non-fiction culture now. A lot of business books, a lot of what's happening in the world, even historical fiction. I think it's important to understand the world around you a bit." Fiction does something non-fiction cannot: it demonstrates world-building rather than describing it. Blood Meridian by Cormac McCarthy is not about historical facts — "it's written in [a way that] he's submerging you immediately in this gritty world from the way that it's written to the actions that are happening, every decision around this submersed you inside this life." To understand how to build a world for a brand, you need to have experienced world-building from the inside.

The three body problem is specifically recommended for Western creatives who do work in Asian markets or want to understand genuinely different worldviews: "Western science fiction books have a protagonist and a hero and someone that works against it. In this book, the people are the hero." The difference in narrative structure is a window into a genuinely different way of thinking.

### Reading Contrarian Material to Sharpen Thinking

"The books that I have been really into recently are all for me about shaping the way I think and approach problems." The recommended current reading practice is explicitly contrarian — reading things that challenge your prior positions not because you agree with the conclusions but because "the way with which they argue, the way with which they talk, how they present the alternatives, and the way people's minds work and approach it is just a really fascinating thing."

"Being able to have your own schools of position or critical thinking — how can I approach this problem? How would I look at this from a contrarian perspective? What principle of psychology can I use here? — it'll allow you to function in an era where people are just thinking dumber than ever." The competitive advantage in the AI era is not information; it is the capacity to think in ways that AI summarizes poorly — nuanced, contextual, genuinely original positioning.

## The Books: What Each One Teaches

### Category 1: Design and Advertising as Cultural Force

**Capslock** (author unnamed in transcript)
The most impactful book in terms of lens installation. Looks at graphic design's impact on capitalism — from currency design to credit cards to modern UX to tourism and the selling of cities. "It is probably one of the most thorough comprehensive looks at the power of how we graphically present the world and how everything around us is designed to sell us on a concept or a thing." Teaches: every visual decision is a power decision. Graphic design is not decoration — it is the architecture of how people experience economic and social reality. Recommended approach: don't read linearly. Pick any section and find the relevant insight.

**Like Art** by Glenn O'Brien
A collection of O'Brien's columns from Art Forum — a sophisticated, nuanced critique of the world of selling things, written when copy mattered and every medium was being invented in real time. "He basically takes a sophisticated, nuanced look at the world of selling things and treats it kind of like the ridiculous beast that it is." Teaches: advertising and creative culture have always been somewhat absurd, and the most insightful observers have always been willing to say so. Good for developing a critical, unsentimental relationship with the creative industries you work in.

**Emotion by Design** (former CMO of Nike, unnamed in transcript)
A breakdown of the "left-brain big brand concept things that they actually work through putting a brand like that together." Teaches: how large-scale brand thinking actually works from the inside — the systematic, almost counter-intuitive process behind emotionally resonant brand decisions.

### Category 2: Context and Oral Histories

**Powerhouse: The Untold Story of Hollywood's Creative Artists Agency** (oral history)
The history of CAA — the talent management agency that changed how Hollywood functioned. An oral history format: "they will basically go interview 50 people from a time period and have them tell the story through their own words." Teaches: "This book describes a turning point in how artists were managed, how Hollywood actors pursued their careers and how that generation of talent worked. This generation of talent is being actively replaced — it's almost complete with the influencer generation." The parallel to the current shift in creative economies is direct.

**These Guys Have All the Fun** (oral history of ESPN)
The story of ESPN changing television and cable, "bullying everyone around because they had sports," from Bristol, Connecticut. Teaches: the current disruption of cable/streaming is the second version of a disruption that ESPN itself caused. Understanding the first round makes the second more legible. Also: how a media institution built on content curation and personality becomes indispensable, and then obsolete.

**The Best of Grantland** (anthology, multiple volumes)
"Truly well-written stuff by people that are passionate and care about fandom" — the best-of collection from Bill Simmons's sports and culture journalism platform. Teaches: what long-form, passion-driven writing actually looks like. Grantland is described as "the last pinnacle of journalism." Recommended for anyone whose content consumption has been entirely algorithm-fed, as a re-immersion in writing that is genuinely excellent rather than optimized for clicks.

**Fear and Loathing on the Campaign Trail '72** by Hunter S. Thompson
Thompson follows the 1972 presidential campaign (McGovern vs. Nixon). Teaches: "how crazy politics and the way people write about politics and interpret it and the way the US has been for so long — where we're at now is just no different than anything was in the '70s." A perspective book for creatives who find themselves consumed by political anxiety. The level of nuance and writing is "just missing from our political spectrum right now."

### Category 3: Mindset and Resistance

**The War of Art** by Steven Pressfield
"The basic symbol of this is that if you don't do the things you want to, creatives deal with this all the time." Pressfield defines the force that prevents creative work as "the Resistance" — a named, concrete enemy that can be identified and fought. "I was living this before I read this book." Teaches: procrastination and creative avoidance have a structure, and naming that structure is the first step to defeating it. Personal context: the creator read this as a VP of Marketing, about to have a child, recognizing that he wasn't doing the creative work he wanted to do despite having no external obstacles.

**Gates of Fire** by Steven Pressfield
The historical novel that 300 is roughly based on. Recommended to be read alongside The War of Art: "This guy who is at the top of his craft, incredible author writing historical fiction, dealt so deeply with procrastination, with his career, with his artistic vision while banging out absolute masterpieces." Teaches: the context of who is giving the creative advice matters. Pressfield is not a self-help author who happened to write about creativity — he is a working writer who learned these lessons while producing genuinely great work. Also: historical fiction about stakes, sacrifice, and the decisions of people who tried to do great things and failed is good for your brain.

**The Obstacle Is the Way** by Ryan Holiday
Stoic philosophy applied to modern life. "The answer to all of them was the obstacle is the way." Teaches: the things you are most avoiding — the phone call you don't want to make, the video you don't want to put your face on, the sales conversation you're too good for — are exactly the things you most need to do. "Every single time you're like, 'I'll do anything but that' — the answer to your seeking is to do that thing." Personal context: used as a management tool — the creator kept a stack on his desk and gave it to anyone who complained.

**The Courage to Be Disliked** (authors: Ichiro Kishimi and Fumitake Koga)
Adlerian psychology presented in dialogue form. Teaches: being good at difficult things — managing people, making honest creative decisions, operating as a creator with a public audience — requires not caring about being liked. "To be a good manager, a good boss, or to push people around you to reach greatness, you're not going to be liked." Critical for creators who identify as people pleasers.

**The 4-Hour Work Week** by Tim Ferriss
Explicitly not recommended for achieving its stated goal ("you will not work a 4-hour work week after reading this book"). Teaches: how to design scalable systems, outsource effectively, and think about the structure of a lifestyle business. "If you want to start a one or two person small business and build something on the side or build a lifestyle around something and begin to quantify the time you spend and where it goes, it is still an extremely valuable look at that."

**Meaning at Work** (author unnamed in transcript)
Given by a mentor during a period of creative confusion — working successfully in marketing but feeling disconnected from the creative identity. Teaches: "the role work plays in our life and letting it define us or not and finding meaning in that in the human journey." Critical for creatives navigating the tension between work that is commercially successful and work that is creatively fulfilling.

### Category 4: Fashion and Craft Reference

**House of Errors** (brand book by Errors)
A photo-and-narrative account of the brand from inception: "guy trying to get clothes done... paying Instagram archive pages to post it so he gets his first sales to messing stuff up to overordering to not having collections work to being a powerhouse brand." Teaches: the actual, unglamorous arc of building a serious fashion brand. "This is a far better look at that than a YouTube video being like 'make $100k on your first drop.'"

**Atlas of Modern Clothing** (author/publisher unnamed, obtained at Premier Vision in Paris)
Goes through every clothing category and names all the components — epaulettes, patch pockets, flat collar, belt configurations, specific garment types. Teaches: the vocabulary. "If you're looking for nomenclature in your design journey... it gives you the vernacular to talk to your designers, to talk to your manufacturers." Knowing the name of what you're asking for is the difference between sending a mood board and communicating a specific intention.

**Textileedia** (author/publisher unnamed)
A comprehensive guide to fabrics: "This is chino, this is cavalry twill. Here's what it's commonly used for. They compare fabrics to each other for different things. Talks about different structures of it. Different printing styles and techniques, dying steps, how dying works." Teaches: the difference between understanding what you're making and "just sending mood board picks and hoping for the best." A foundation for direct, technical communication with manufacturers.

### Category 5: Business and Operations

**$100 Million Offers** by Alex Hormozi
"Changed my life, will probably change yours." Teaches: how to structure what you sell — moving from "I am available for freelance work" to packaging skills into a defined offer with specific value delivery and repeat mechanisms. "A lot of creatives want to do freelance work. You want to design, you want to do creative direction, doing social media management, you make websites, whatever. This book teaches you how to package that into a viable thing people want to get and repeat." Teaches sales psychology: guarantees, extras, positioning. "If you just follow half of it, you're going to sell better than 90% of creatives."

**Sponsor Magnet** by Justin (former student of the creator's community)
How to get brand sponsorships: reaching out, preparing proposals, structuring the relationship. "The art of selling yourself. If you don't have a manager, if you're just trying to think through more of how do I get money from my online audience? Excellent one."

**Becoming Trader Joe's** (author unnamed in transcript)
An analysis of how every decision at Trader Joe's — packaging, pricing, store layout, employee compensation — exists for a deliberate reason that serves a coherent positioning strategy. Teaches: what immaculately thought-through positioning looks like in a business context. "I think a lot of marketers would do really well to read this."

**Super Pumped** (the Uber story, author unnamed)
The story of Travis Kalanick and Uber's rise. "This book was meant as a cautionary tale" but functions as something more ambiguous. Teaches: aggressive, ego-driven brand building — what it looks like, how it works, where it breaks. "If I did this with slightly less ego, this is kind of the blueprint for aggressively building something."

**Exponential Organizations** (author unnamed in transcript)
The concept of a business that grows "radically more than the sum of its parts" — hockey-stick potential that exists beyond headcount scaling. Cliffnotes or audiobook recommended. Teaches: the concept of exponential growth, not the how-to.

**Made to Stick** by Chip and Dan Heath
"A book about messaging... when you launch a product or you launch a thing it having positioning and angle and messaging that works to get people to care about ideas." Teaches: the principles of sticky communication — ideas that are simple, unexpected, concrete, credible, emotional, and story-driven. "If you are a marketer or you're trying to position something... you need to be able to communicate from the start you're making something different and know how to position it."

**Traction** (EOS / Entrepreneurial Operating System, by Gino Wickman)
A framework for running businesses with more than 10 people: goal-setting structures, communication systems between executive and operational layers. "You don't realize there are structures that exist that are built really well that I can just run my company on." Teaches: operational infrastructure for creative businesses that have grown past founder-can-manage-everything scale.

### Category 6: Contrarian / Critical Thinking (Current Reads)

**Gray Mirror** — Curtis Yarvin (Mencius Moldbug)
The host's current read at time of recording. "Positions a lot of common liberal perceptions of the past and presents counterpoints to them." Recommended not for ideological agreement but as a study in argument construction: "The way with which they argue, the way with which they talk, how they present the alternatives, and the way people's minds work and approach it is just a really fascinating thing." The stated purpose: in an AI era where ChatGPT and Claude synthesize consensus answers, "being able to have your own schools of position or critical thinking" is the last form of genuine creative differentiation. Teaches: how contrarian arguments are built, how to position unpopular views compellingly, and how to think about problems from first principles rather than from inherited frameworks.

### Category 7: World-Building and Fiction

**Zerville** and **The Adderall Diaries** (authors unnamed)
"Very American fiction books of previous generations of America and the human emotion and people going through them." Teaches: "How would you write or interpret the life that you're going through now or went through over the last few years in a way like this? It's an interesting way to set up a world for a modern brand."

**Blood Meridian** by Cormac McCarthy
Set on the Texas-Mexico border in the 1850s. "He's submerging you immediately in this gritty world from the way that it's written to the actions that are happening, every decision around this submerges you inside this life." Teaches: immersive world-building through language. "How you can apply kind of those same principles to building your world." Warning: this is a dark book.

**The Three-Body Problem** by Liu Cixin
Chinese science fiction, translated to English. The structural difference from Western sci-fi: "Western science fiction books have a protagonist and a hero and someone that works against it. In this book, the people are the hero." Teaches: a genuinely different worldview — collective rather than individual heroism. A conduit to understanding how people in different parts of the world think, especially valuable for anyone doing creative work in or for Asian markets.

## Key Insights

1. **The goal of reading is the lens, not the information.** Every book you read installs a framework for seeing — or fails to. Choose books for the quality of the lens they provide, not for the facts they contain.

2. **Algorithm-curated content produces convergent reference.** Books are one of the few remaining tools that can give you a genuinely different perspective than your peer group, because books are selected deliberately rather than served by a machine that is optimizing for engagement.

3. **Read across categories.** A creative whose reading is entirely within their discipline is building a narrow mental model library. The orally historical, literary, political, philosophical, and operational readings produce a mind that sees connections across domains — which is the foundation of original creative thinking.

4. **The reading practice is the key, not the volume.** 30 minutes before sleep, off screens. One book per month, occasionally two. Consistently maintained over years, this produces a library of mental models that compounds. The volume matters less than the consistency.

5. **Difficult books should be skipped through, not avoided.** Dense, foundational works are not meant to be read cover-to-cover on a first pass. Open any section and find the relevant insight. Return when you have a specific problem the book addresses.

6. **Read contrarian material to sharpen your thinking.** The goal is not to be persuaded — it is to understand how strong arguments for positions you disagree with are constructed. This teaches critical thinking more effectively than reading material you already agree with.
