# Concept: Starbridge Speedrun — Call-by-Call Analysis
tags: [cold calling, B2B, outbound, Starbridge, call analysis, objection handling, qualification, coaching, rep training]

## Summary / Definition

A call-by-call breakdown of every meaningful conversation in Micah's 7.5-hour Starbridge cold call speedrun. Each call is analyzed for: what happened, what Micah did right, what he did wrong, and what the exchange reveals about the product, the ICP, or the craft of cold calling. This is a rep training document — specific enough to use as coaching material.

Source: YouTube video tdYvRKVr0pk (56 chunks, 134 min). Calls run from chunk 10 through chunk 54.

Session result: 770 dials → 50 pickups → 33 conversations → 16 right-ICP conversations → 6 bookings confirmed → 1 deal qualified and paid ($1,500 confirmed on Stripe, day 2).

### Call 1: Megan (Chunks 13-14) — NOT ICP / Bad Data

**Company:** CCI (unspecified)
**What happened:** Micah calls for "Megan." The person who answers says "she doesn't work here anymore" — but then clarifies she IS still there, she's just the one answering. Weird situation. Micah pivots: "Oh, you are? Okay, gotcha. Yeah, name's Micah..." Gives the permission opener. Megan gets defensive immediately: "I just can't really answer questions about our business without really knowing like who you are, why you're calling. I get a lot of these calls."

Micah gives the full thumbnail pitch including SLED focus, intent signals, board meeting minutes, contract expirations. Megan: "I'm not interested at the time, but I can see what you do. I don't see a direct use case for what we do, but if you send me an email summary, I can get back to you if anything changes." Sends to Megan's old email on file.

**What went wrong:**
- The opener created confusion because the person who answered was not clearly identified. "I was looking for Megan" when the person answering IS Megan (or someone who knows her) created a weird dynamic from the jump.
- Micah deviated from his own rule — he pitched before getting a clear pre-qualifier confirmation. Megan never said "yes, we sell to SLED." He gave the full pitch to a semi-qualified, defensive prospect.
- The "send me an email" close is a soft rejection wrapped in politeness. She's giving him a way out that lets her feel like she was nice.

**Micah's self-assessment:** "That was really weird... not relevant persona. I'm not a huge fan of asking find-out questions early on... All right, I need to just clean this up."

**The lesson:** When the opener creates confusion (wrong Megan, right Megan, unclear handoff), reset to zero. Get the name confirmed and pre-qualify before spending a single word on the pitch. The defensive "I get a lot of these calls" response is a signal that the opener didn't land cleanly — she didn't feel the permission was genuine because the interaction was already off-track.

### Call 2: Antonio (Chunks 14-15) — NOT ICP, Clean Exit

**Company:** Unknown
**What happened:** Clean permission opener. Pre-qualifier: "Do you guys sell into SLED accounts like state, local, education?" Antonio: "Rarely. That's not really..." Micah hears "not really" and decides to give a short thumbnail anyway: "This might not be super relevant, but maybe I'll give you the 10-second thumbnail version and just cut me off if it's outside of your swim lane." Gives the full pitch. Antonio says they only go after government occasionally. Not a priority.

At the end, Antonio says: "I want to be aware in case SLED ever becomes relevant — but nothing indicates that right now." Micah: "For sure. For sure. Okay." Clean exit.

**What worked:**
- Pre-qualifier landed cleanly and identified the DQ quickly.
- Giving a short pitch after "rarely" was defensible — "rarely" is not "never." The pitch was brief and Micah offered the "just in case" exit the whole way through.
- The exit was clean: "Okay. Well, that's I appreciate the call." No push, no follow-up ask.

**What could have been better:**
- When someone says "rarely," the better move might be: "How much of your revenue comes from SLED — is it 5%? 20%?" That's a more specific qualifier than guessing from "rarely." Micah pitched without knowing if "rarely" means 1% or 30%.

**The lesson:** "Rarely" is an ambiguous qualifier. The pre-qualifier question ("do you sell to SLED?") needs a follow-up for soft yeses and soft nos. "Rarely" → "What percentage of your revenue comes from that space?" gives you real data to decide whether to pitch.

### Call 4: Chris (Chunks 15-16) — Bad Data / Wrong Person

**Company:** Pulse CFO / Spring Health

**What worked:**
- The exit was immediate and gracious the moment he confirmed it was the wrong person. No push, no confusion.

### Call 5: Sonia (Chunks 16-18) — CLOSE MISS / Existing Tool + Timing

**Company:** Unknown (sells to school districts)
**What happened:** This is the most instructive near-miss of the session. Sonia says "We settle school districts" — clear ICP. Micah runs the full pitch. Then Sonia drops the bomb:

"Yeah, I've tried both of those [ZoomInfo and GovSpend] and none of them worked for me."

This is a GIFT. A prospect who has tried competitors and been burned is a perfect Starbridge candidate — she has proof of pain. Micah responds: "Neither of those worked for you. We hear that quite often. So that's kind of why we're doing things a little different. Would you have any open slots maybe tomorrow afternoon?"

But then Sonia explains the real reason they didn't work: "I don't know if intent really works for us. Because it's government — you go after every single signal that's happening with school districts, and I can't use that. I need to know who's looking for routing software. Period. That's it. It's more of a who's searching for specific long-tail keywords. And I'm doing that already with another company called Pearl Divers."

Sonia needs **keyword-intent** (who is searching now) not **buying-signal intent** (who is likely to buy based on context). These are fundamentally different. Starbridge can't replace Pearl Divers for what Sonia needs.

Micah tries to recover — "What you mentioned with targeting specific people with keywords is actually something we help with too" — but it's too late. Sonia has a meeting in two minutes. She exits: "If you want to check in next year, maybe things will change then."

**What went wrong:**
- Micah didn't ask the follow-up question fast enough: "Neither worked — what were you actually hoping they'd do?" That one question reveals whether Sonia's pain is solvable by Starbridge.
- The recovery attempt ("we help with keywords and phrases too") was a stretch. Starbridge's keyword capability is not its core product. Claiming overlap to save the call undermined his credibility.

**What worked:**
- The opener was solid. The pre-qualifier landed correctly.
- The "neither worked for you — we hear that quite often" response was excellent framing that could have led somewhere good if Sonia had more time.
- Micah's self-assessment is accurate: "I just didn't say the magic words." The magic words would have been: "What were you hoping GovSpend would surface that it didn't?"

**Micah's real-time debrief:** "That one wasn't like she was uninterested. I think she just had a meeting. So she's like 'come on, come on.' Yeah, we'll definitely catch her another time."

**The lesson:** "We tried your category and it didn't work" is not an objection — it's an invitation to understand what they actually needed. The deeper question ("what were you hoping to find?") is the move. Getting defensive or claiming capability overlap kills trust faster than losing the call.

**Additional lesson:** Pearl Divers is a named competitor worth knowing. It targets a different buyer motion (keyword/search intent) vs. Starbridge (contextual buying signals). Some SLED sellers need the former, some need the latter — the qualification question should eventually probe for which type of intent they're looking for.

### Call 6: Ellie / Sentinel Maintenance (Chunks 18-21) — BOOKING #1

**Company:** Sentinel Maintenance — facility maintenance, Denver HQ, national (20 states)
**What happened:** Clean permission opener. "Yes" to SLED. Full pitch. Ellie asks one qualifying question: "Do you guys cover nationwide or are you region specific?" → Nationwide US. Ellie says "You have time maybe like tomorrow, Friday or next Monday?" Books for 9am Central (she's in Minneapolis traveling).

Micah sends Calendly on the call, asks to hit accept. Then asks: "I do know some folks like to hone in on one or two specific areas depending on what's holding them hostage. Maybe it's wasted time chasing low-intent accounts, maybe missing RFP windows... But I guess for your team, what's been the biggest roadblock lately?"

Ellie: "We just had an inside salesperson and it wasn't generating enough meetings per week and he left recently. So we're kind of looking for a platform that will kind of be all-encompassing."

**What went exceptionally well:**
- The roadblock question after booking is underrated. It's asked AFTER the calendar invite is accepted — which means it's pure intelligence gathering, not qualification pressure. Ellie gives a detailed answer because she's already committed. Micah gets context to prep Zach for the handoff.
- Ellie's situation is textbook ICP: lost the inside sales function, actively evaluating platforms, mandate from boss to vet vendors. She's in buying mode. Micah identifies this immediately: "That's got to be primo for sure."
- The Calendly sent while she's jumping out of an Uber in Minneapolis — shows the on-call booking works even in chaotic circumstances.

**What could have been sharper:**
- Micah asks "anyone else you want to loop in?" and Ellie says "just me for now, boss wants me to vet." This is worth a follow-up: "Got it — and just so I can help prep, does your boss have a title or a specific angle he's looking for?" Preps the handoff better.

**Micah's post-call notes (he writes these up for the team):**
- 6 outside sales + 6 proposal team
- Looking for all-encompassing tool
- Boss mandated her to vet vendors
- Targets government facilities, public libraries, schools/universities
- Denver HQ, 20 states

**The lesson:** Booking first, then asking the roadblock question is a smarter sequence than using the roadblock question as a pre-booking qualifier. Once the calendar is accepted, the conversation transforms — from sales to consultation. Ellie gave much richer detail after confirming than she would have before.

### Call 8: Christian's Replacement → Kyle at 911 Restoration (Chunks 21-24) — BOOKING #2

**Company:** 911 Restoration (restoration services — water, fire, biohazard cleanup)
**What happened:** One of the most technically impressive moments in the video. Micah calls for "Christian" — the listed bizdev manager. Gatekeeper: "I do apologize. Christian is not with us anymore. However, I can help you with whatever Christian would do for you." Micah immediately pivots: "Oh, gotcha. Okay. Do you know who took his place as the bizdev manager?" Gatekeeper: "Yep. Let me connect you now with him." Connects to Kyle.

Kyle answers. Doesn't know Starbridge ("he didn't turn in his book of business when he left, so I don't know all of his connections"). Micah: "Okay. Well, then I can give you the quick rundown." Full pitch. Kyle: "Yeah, but not tomorrow afternoon. I would be able to do... tomorrow's Wednesday, not Thursday. So I could do tomorrow afternoon. What do you have?" Books for noon Eastern.

After booking, Micah asks the roadblock question. Kyle: "I don't particularly have a business development representative, so I need something that will assist me in my efforts until I can replace the position. It's a lot for me working for multiple branches... A lot of places are very much like right when you come in they immediately shut you down. So we've been trying to approach from inside sales... knowing when contracts expire is huge — that gives us the perfect time window."

**What went exceptionally well:**
- The gatekeeper-to-replacement pivot is flawless. Micah doesn't accept "Christian is gone" as a dead end. He asks for the name of the replacement in the same breath. No gap, no hesitation.
- Kyle's pain points line up perfectly with Starbridge's value prop: no BDR, getting shut out by gatekeepers, needs contract expiry data. The product was genuinely a fit.
- Micah's insight in the post-call debrief: "The highlight for that deal is it was a gatekeeper that said the contact was not available because that contact was no longer there. Me asking for the new replacement, me talking to new replacement and finding out there's a need and then booking them for a meeting. You just always got to find your deal."

**The lesson:** "This person is no longer here" is not a dead end — it's an opportunity. The replacement inherits the predecessor's contacts, relationships, and tools but often doesn't know what was in motion. That creates an opening: you're not a cold stranger, you're someone trying to connect with the team. Always ask who took over.

### Call 10: Sean (Chunks 28-32) — BOOKING #4

**Company:** Unknown — 34 years in business, education + government + 5 other verticals, higher ed focus
**What happened:** The most challenging successful booking of the session. Sean sells to education and government, but he pushes back hard on the product.

After the full pitch, Sean asks: "Are these just webinars or are they scheduled meetings?" Micah clarifies: scheduled meetings, relevant to exactly what they're looking at. Sean then drills into K12 vs. higher education (they're higher ed). Micah confirms higher ed is supported.

Sean then pushes back on the product itself: "We have ZoomInfo. We have SAM.gov for our bids. It's not like there's a lack of resources. It's sometimes we're a very niche business... We've been around 34 years. Contractors send bids to us directly. We're not having to hunt. Knowing that ZoomInfo costs a fortune..."

This is the "established player" objection. They're not hunting because they don't have to. Micah doesn't fold: "We're not a fit for every team and sounds like you guys obviously have some pretty robust motions going on. I guess if there was one or two areas we could lend a hand..."

But Sean stops him: "I'd rather have some info sent before I jump on a call. You called me out of the blue. I actually was thinking it was a different call from a different place." Micah takes the info-first path. Sends a one-pager + calendar link. After seeing the website, Sean's interest shifts: "You're not just after the fact — you're finding available RFPs... you could also find out what competitors bid after the fact." Books for Wednesday the 24th at noon (working around his golf tournament).

**What went right:**
- Micah didn't abandon the call when Sean said "we have ZoomInfo and SAM.gov." He acknowledged their strength and looked for a gap — "if there was one or two areas we could lend a hand."
- When Sean asked for info first, Micah gave it willingly and combined it with the calendar link. The soft booking (info + calendar) still converted into a hard booking once Sean saw the website.
- Sean's own summary as the call ends reveals the breakthrough: "You're also finding the opportunities beforehand." He got the forward-looking differentiation on his own. The one-pager did the explaining Micah didn't need to.

**What was hard:**
- Sean is a 34-year relationship-driven company that doesn't hunt because inbound works fine. Starbridge's value prop (find accounts before RFPs drop) is less urgent for a company that gets direct bids. The meeting was booked, but qualification on this one is questionable — they're not actively hungry for more accounts.

**The lesson:** "We already have this workflow" from a well-established company is the hardest objection because it might actually be true. The way through is to not challenge the workflow but to find the edge case where their current approach has a gap. In Sean's case: "Are there any verticals or geographies where you're trying to grow that your current network doesn't cover?" That would reveal whether Starbridge is additive.

### Call 13: Judson → Eric Referral (Chunks 33-35) — REFERRAL CONVERT / BOOKING #5

**Company:** Robert Energy (Judson now at Hayden)
**What happened:** Two-stage play. Stage one: Judson is no longer at Robert Energy (bad data). But Micah doesn't just mark it and move. He engages: "I know Robert Energy does do a lot of work in government and the military. So I can definitely get you a point of contact if you need it."

Judson: "His name is Eric." Gives the phone number directly.

Stage two: Micah calls Eric. Completely different opener: "Hey, good afternoon, Eric. It's Micah over at Starbridge. Our mutual connection, Jetson [he says Jetson, referring to Judson], told me to darken your door. I'll blame him for the interruption, but do you mind if I grab half a minute here?"

Eric: "Yeah, for sure." 

Micah checks if Starbridge rings a bell — it doesn't. Full pitch. Eric: "Yeah, I could probably do that tomorrow afternoon." Books for 1:30 Eastern. On-call Calendly. Micah then qualifies: "Does Starbridge ring a bell? Does your company have defense contracts and SLED?" Yes. Bottleneck: "Access to all the RFPs and submitting them in a timely manner — they run through different hosting platforms." Solo decision maker.

**What went exceptionally well:**
- The referral conversion is the most technically sophisticated move in the entire session. Micah turned a bad data call (Judson) into a warm referral (Eric) in the same interaction.
- The opener on Eric's call is meaningfully different: "Our mutual connection told me to darken your door. I'll blame him for the interruption." This opener is not a cold call — it's a referral call. Different psychology, different tone. Eric said yes before Micah even stated why he was calling.
- Eric's pain ("RFPs are across different hosting platforms") maps directly to one of Starbridge's core features (aggregated RFP sourcing). The qualification and the pitch were already aligned before Micah knew it.

**The coaching moment Micah calls out:** "It was another great example of a deal where the person on file was wrong. I got the referral. I connected with the referral and found the deal. That's really the name of the game. Pass the ball. Boom. Assist."

**The lesson:** Always ask the disqualified contact for a referral. The question is simple: "Do you know who on your team or at another company handles this more directly?" You're not hoping they'll give someone up — you're giving them an easy way to be helpful. Judson gave a name and a direct number. That's the most efficient pipeline generation in the session. One bad data call → one warm referral → one booked meeting.

### Call 14: Leah (Chunks 36-38) — EXISTING TOOL CONVERT / BOOKING #6

**Company:** Small firm, <50 people, K12 and higher education focus
**What happened:** Leah is a consultant — her firm works under architects who hold the prime contract with government agencies. She has one person handling everything: herself. Micah gives the pitch and then Leah stops him:

"We already have a contract with that, and I'm not sure when we renew. I think it's sometime in January. We usually do two-year contracts. So that would be a competitor, correct?"

This is the existing tool objection in its cleanest form. The competitor is unnamed but clearly a similar product. Leah is asking if Starbridge competes.

Micah: "Yeah. No, this is pretty similar... there could definitely be some overlap. Definitely don't want to rip and replace, especially if it's working well for you guys. I do know a lot of folks like to do these like apples-to-apples comparisons just because we have, you know, we're kind of that new wave of AI capability. Don't want to waste your time. Would it be a ridiculous idea to kind of share our capabilities and then from there maybe keep us in the back pocket for the renewal time?"

Leah: "Yeah, that's fine. I don't have any time this week, but maybe the following week." Books for Tuesday at 3.

Leah also asks: "Would you mind sending me your website and if you have a schedule of fees or different packages? I find it easier if I have all my options... We're a small firm. Less than 50 people." Micah: "I think I'm personally not authorized to give price sheets, but let me see if I can have my director send something over in advance."

**What went right:**
- The "keep us in the back pocket for renewal" reframe is perfect. It doesn't force a decision — it just opens a window. Leah's renewal is in January. Starbridge will be in her consideration set when that comes around. That's the win.
- "Would it be a ridiculous idea" is a specific framing technique — it preemptively handles the objection by making refusal seem unreasonable. If it's not a ridiculous idea, say yes. It's nearly impossible to say no to.
- The pricing deflection ("I'm not authorized to give price sheets") is handled correctly. He doesn't make up a number, he doesn't dodge — he says his director will handle it. This creates a reason for the handoff and keeps Zach's credibility intact for the meeting.

**The lesson:** "We already have a tool" is not a no — it's a timing issue disguised as a product objection. The right response is not to challenge the existing tool. It's to reduce the stakes of the interaction: "I just want you to know your options. You don't have to switch anything." A January renewal window means Micah has 3-6 months to become the comparison standard. He just bought that window with a 3-minute call.

### Call 16: Burke (Chunks 39-40) — ADJACENT ICP / Gracious DQ

**Company:** Dental equipment and training (46 dental universities in North America)
**What happened:** Burke says "We have a full government strike force." ICP confirmed. Micah pitches. Mid-pitch, Burke interrupts: "Sorry, when you say K12 you mean K through 12?" Micah: "K through 12, yeah." Burke: "I don't even deal with that. I'm dealing with university accounts specifically, specifically dental residency programs."

Micah pivots: "Nice. We definitely help with universities as well. As long as they're government funded." Continues pitch. Burke then explains his situation: "In the dental business, there's only 46 dental universities in North America and we have a contract with every single one of them. If you're a dental school, we're training on everything that we make... we don't really need it. If we make it, they use it."

His model: market capture is already complete. There's no hunting needed because they own every account. Starbridge is irrelevant when you already have 100% market penetration.

Burke is genuinely gracious: "I wanted to give you that [time] but I just don't think we're a good fit from what I do." He even compliments Micah: "I wish you the best of luck. It sounds like you have a neat product."

Micah responds: "Yeah, I feel like in a lot of ways we're taking this vertical by storm. Sometimes we miss the mark like in this case, but it's a little fuzzy — yes you work with government or SLED, but like you said, it's kind of an uncommon model."

**What went right:**
- Micah listened through Burke's explanation and correctly identified the business model mismatch — not just "doesn't sell to SLED" but "sells to SLED but doesn't need to hunt." These are different DQ reasons and should be logged differently.
- Micah's self-aware comment ("sometimes we miss the mark like in this case — it's a little fuzzy") shows he's learning about the ICP edges in real time.

**What's interesting:**
- Burke is the ideal prospect on paper: government strike force, works with universities. But the qualifier question "do you sell into SLED?" doesn't surface the model. Burke sells equipment TO the dental school, the dental school doesn't buy FROM a competitive RFP process — there's no procurement competition. A better qualifier: "Do you actively bid against competitors for those contracts, or are you the established vendor?" would surface this faster.

**The lesson:** ICP qualification needs a second layer for established market leaders. "Do you sell to SLED?" surfaces category. "Do you compete for those accounts or are you the incumbent?" surfaces whether they actually have the pain Starbridge solves. Burke had zero pain — his market was sewn up. That's a fundamentally different DQ than "SLED isn't a priority for us."

### Call 18: Chad (Chunks 40-41) — DNC

**What happened:** Chad picks up. Micah starts the opener. Chad: "If it's a sales call, it's probably not relevant. I'm not in the market for anything. Take me off the list. Thanks." Micah: "Interesting. Do not call. Requested DNC." Zero pushback. Immediate mark.

**What worked:**
- DNC requests are honored instantly. No "just one thing," no "I understand but," no second pitch attempt. The moment the words "take me off the list" are spoken, the call is over.
- Micah's tone after: clinical, not offended. "Interesting. Do not call." He moves on immediately.

**The lesson:** Chad's response is actually a reasonable one for a busy person — "I'm not in the market, please don't call again." Honoring that request immediately is the only correct response. Any attempt to continue the call after a DNC request destroys credibility and possibly violates regulations. The correct emotion is neutral. The correct action is log and move.

### Call 19: Joanna (Chunk 41) — NOT ICP, Prior Contact

**Company:** Construction (Dayton area)
**What happened:** Joanna says she thinks she talked to a colleague from Starbridge 2-3 months ago and "we concluded that this was not a fit. We don't sell into those organizations."

Micah confirms with LinkedIn context: "I'm looking at your LinkedIn — you guys obviously do construction. But you're not building for like schools or universities or like cities or state agencies?"

Joanna: "No, we do some of that but usually through private clients. We don't work a lot through municipalities directly. Our federal work through the government is done through the Corps of Engineers. So we don't feel like we need anything for that."

**What worked:**
- Micah referenced LinkedIn while on the call to verify — he caught a potential mismatch and named what he saw: "you do construction but you're not building for government directly." That's the right question.
- When the prior contact confirmed the DQ from 3 months ago, Micah accepted the verdict. No re-pitching of an already-disqualified contact.

**The lesson:** Repeat call on a DQ is a list quality problem. The CRM should have logged the prior disqualification with a reason and a do-not-call flag for this contact's company. The fact that Joanna received a second call 3 months after a confirmed DQ means the data cleanup didn't happen. This is a platform hygiene issue, not a calling issue.

### Call 20: Jamie (Chunks 41-43) — WRONG CONTACT, INFO SEND

**Company:** Unknown (west coast, Arizona time)
**What happened:** Jamie picks up. "I believe so" they sell to SLED. Micah gives the full pitch. Jamie: "I don't make those decisions. That's my director of sales." She's in the service department, not sales.

It's also 6pm in her timezone. "She would be able to make those decisions. I do not." Micah asks for an email to forward info. Jamie gives the general info@ address and says Amanda (director) will see it.

**What went right:**
- Micah immediately asks for the right path when Jamie says she's not the decision maker. He doesn't try to convert her — he tries to route correctly.
- Getting the director's name (Amanda) is good intel even if the send goes to a general inbox.

**What could have been sharper:**
- "Send info to info@" is a cold send to an inbox Amanda might check or might ignore. A better move: "Is there a direct email for Amanda? I want to make sure it hits her specifically and not a general inbox." Direct email is 5x more likely to get seen.
- Micah also missed a follow-up question: "If I reach out to Amanda directly, would it be okay if I reference your name?" That warms the cold send significantly.

**The lesson:** When routed to a general inbox, always try to get the decision maker's direct email and permission to use the current contact's name. "Sent info to info@" is functionally a dead send unless someone at the company is diligent about forwarding.

### Warm Handoff 1: Nicole / Ellie — Sentinel Maintenance (Chunks 44-48)

**Company:** Facility management/maintenance, 7 outside sales reps, 20 states, Denver HQ
**What happened:** Director Zach runs the demo. Nicole (the day-2 name used — possibly same Ellie) is at a facilities conference in Minneapolis. She mentions the facility management conference (IFMA). Zach does discovery: "Tell me about your sales motion today." She describes: mostly outside field sales, trying to find what platforms can supplement since losing inside sales.

Zach asks: "What are you using today?" Atlas ("kind of like ZoomInfo but data") + NetSuite CRM. Then: "How much of your business is public sector vs. commercial?" She does a lot of public sector in Denver, mostly commercial/private everywhere else.

Zach shows the platform. After the demo she asks about next steps. Wants to send materials to talk to her boss.

**Micah's post-call assessment:** "It looks like it should have been pre-qualified because her title was business development manager, but sometimes companies make bisdev manager like they're actually just an individual contributor." He's worried about two things: (1) She's primarily commercial private sector, the SLED team is separate. (2) No clear timeline. He says he might dispute this one if it doesn't qualify.

**What the call reveals about Starbridge's sales process:**
- The warm handoff sequence requires re-qualifying on the discovery call what Micah qualified on the cold call. That's intentional — Micah's cold call is a quick pass/fail gate, not full discovery.
- When Zach asks "how much of your business is public sector vs. commercial?" he's doing the real ICP validation. Micah only verified that the company sells to SLED — Zach verifies that SLED is a significant enough piece to justify the tool.
- This is the structural weakness Micah identifies: he should have drilled harder on "what percentage of revenue comes from SLED?" before booking. "Do you sell to SLED" gets a yes/no. "What percentage?" gets a number that tells you if it's worth handoff.

**The lesson for qualification:** Two-stage ICP validation. Stage 1 (cold call): "Do you sell to SLED?" Stage 2 (warm handoff): "What percentage of revenue is SLED, and who on your team actively works those accounts?" Without Stage 2, you hand off deals that look qualified but aren't.

### Warm Handoff 2: Kyle — 911 Restoration (Chunks 48-54) — QUALIFIED / DEAL PAID

**Company:** 911 Restoration — biohazard, fire, water damage restoration
**What happened:** This is the most successful call in the entire session. Kyle is actually Kyle's supervisor — Kyle the original contact (from the cold call) was replaced by this person. The BDR (Christian) is gone. This Kyle is a supervisor filling in.

Zach does discovery: "What are you doing today to develop new business?" Kyle describes: field visits, referral fees to plumbers/HVAC companies ($500 for qualified leads, $100 for mentions), inside sales outreach. Primary goal: get into fire departments for board-up contracts after fires. Problem: fire departments are all locked into contracts with major vendors (Belfor, ServPro). He walked into the right fire department 3 days before a fire — pure luck.

Zach runs the live demo. Searches "fire department modernization initiatives" and shows results. Shows account scoring, purchase order data, contact lists, grant funding alerts. Kyle: "I apologize because I kind of got into this but I didn't know that our BM was already talking to you guys. But now that I've seen it, this looks like it's going to save us quite a bit of time and energy. Like you said, this is something I could do inside sales too."

Pricing: ~$15K/year annual agreement. Kyle asks about payment structure. Zach offers 50% down + quarterly. Kyle: "I like that. I like it."

Kyle's supervisor (who was on the call) gets qualified. Deal confirmed on Stripe at 10am. $1,500 payout (first of potentially 6, rest pending).

**What made this the winning call:**
1. **The demo was built around Kyle's exact use case.** Zach didn't show a generic SLED demo — he searched fire departments specifically, showed what Starbridge finds for fire departments, scored fire departments Kyle should prioritize. Kyle saw himself in the platform immediately.
2. **Kyle's pain was real and unsolved.** Random showups to fire departments hoping to get lucky before a fire happens is exactly the problem Starbridge solves. The transition from "I walked into the right place 3 days before a fire" to "we'll tell you which fire departments are talking about procurement in the last 30 days" is a 10-to-1 efficiency gain.
3. **The surprise factor converted Kyle.** "I apologize — I didn't know our BM was already talking to you" is the moment Kyle went from attendee to buyer. He felt slightly behind on a product his predecessor had already been evaluating. That created urgency.
4. **Payment flexibility closed the deal.** $15K upfront would have been a friction point for a small restoration business. 50% down + quarterly made it digestible.

**What the failed first handoff (Nicole) teaches in contrast:**
Nicole was a 7-rep facility management company that does SLED but she's on the commercial side personally. The platform would help her team's RFP department — but she's not in it, doesn't feel the pain directly, and her timeline is "early next year." Kyle felt the pain himself (he's the one doing bizdev, he's the one showing up randomly to fire departments). When the person on the call IS the person with the pain, demos convert. When they're evaluating on behalf of a team that's not on the call, demos don't.

**The lesson:** Discovery before demo. The most important discovery question: "Are you personally involved in going after these accounts, or are you evaluating this for your team?" If the answer is "evaluating for a team that isn't here," either get the team on the call or prepare for a slower, harder close.

## Session Debrief: Micah's Own Assessment

At the end of the speedrun (chunk 55), Micah reviews what he'd do differently:

**1. Qualify harder on SLED specificity.**
"I should have asked more specifically — are you the one that handles the SLED accounts? Are you the end decision maker or at least an influencer? And then maybe do you guys have at least five sales folks that actively work on the SLED team."

**2. The bad data rate was manageable.**
24% of pickups were truly wrong (wrong person, retired, changed companies). Not great but workable. Fix: tighter list sourcing with more recent data scraping.

**3. The wrong ICP rate was the real issue.**
34% of pickups were the wrong ICP (don't sell to SLED enough or in the right way). Fix: better pre-qualification question with a follow-up about what percentage of revenue comes from SLED.

**4. Show rate was 100% for the confirmed qualified calls.**
Both calls that showed on day 2 showed up. The on-call Calendly booking system worked.

**5. The campaign itself is hot.**
"One of the hottest ones on the Glen Coco marketplace right now." Demand for Starbridge meetings is real. The $1,000/qualified meeting payout is achievable with the system he ran.
