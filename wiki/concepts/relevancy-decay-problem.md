# Concept: The Relevancy Decay Problem — Why Experts Go Stale and How to Stay Sharp

## Summary

Expertise in marketing, content, and brand strategy has a decay rate of four to six months. Someone who was deeply knowledgeable about platform performance, content formats, and campaign mechanics in early 2025 can be functionally obsolete by late 2025 if they've stepped away from the work. This isn't about learning new tools — it's about losing the texture and specificity that makes expertise credible. The creator who built their authority on insider knowledge of how a 12-person team at a $100M brand operates can't speak to that with authority if they haven't been inside a team like that for a year. The decay is real, and ignoring it is the reason most content creators plateau or lose relevance.

## How Decay Happens

Orin John documented his own decay in real time. After leaving his executive role — where he was managing a 12-14 person marketing team with $100M revenue targets — he noticed within five to six months that he couldn't speak with the same authority. He wasn't running large campaigns anymore. He wasn't dealing with the specific operational problems that his audience faced. His content became slightly less sharp without him realizing it at first.

The mechanism: niche expertise content derives its credibility from lived, current experience. The moment that experience becomes past-tense, the content begins to soften. You can still talk about the principles, but you lose the texture — the specific platform quirks, the current tool stack, the real problems teams are dealing with right now.

## The Four-to-Six Month Rule

A senior marketer who sold a company for $100M+ and stepped away from day-to-day marketing for a year is functionally out of touch in the current environment. The pace of platform change — algorithm updates, new format dominance, shifting creative standards — means that staying current is active work, not passive maintenance. Four to six months of non-immersion is enough to fall behind in a way that audiences can sense even if they can't name it.

## The Fix: Intentional Re-immersion

Orin's solution was deliberate: he went back to working part-time specifically to stay current. Not for income — he makes more as a creator than he did as an executive — but for the sharpness that comes from being in the mix. The part-time work is content infrastructure.

For creators who can't or won't go back to client work, the equivalent is:
- Time-blocked niche research (scheduled scroll sessions, not passive consumption)
- Active experimentation with current tools and formats
- Building systems to capture and organize current references (see: [[creative-toolkit-bookmark-system]])

## The Leveling-Up Imperative

"Everyone on the internet is improving at what they create every day. I'm such a better creator than I was six months ago or even a year ago. So if you're expecting the same thing to work, it's not. You must be leveling up at the same time."

This is the corollary to relevancy decay: the bar is moving up, not just sideways. The content quality that was excellent 18 months ago is average today. Staying in place is falling behind.

## What to Steal

Audit your own relevancy annually: is the expertise you're creating content about still lived, current experience? Or has it become past-tense? If it's drifting toward past-tense, either find a way to re-immerse (consulting, part-time work, active experimentation) or explicitly reframe your content as historical perspective rather than current playbook. Both can work — only the unacknowledged drift is fatal to credibility.
