# Concept: Product Architecture Framework

## Summary

Taylor Holiday's framework for categorizing a DTC product line by performance and potential. Four categories: Champions, Growth Drivers, Underperformers, Hidden Gems. The framework rationalizes SKU strategy and determines which products deserve marketing spend, which should scale, and which should be cut or repositioned. Product line expansion errors are one of the most common ways 7-figure brands destroy their own profitability.

---

## The Four Categories

| Category | Description | Marketing Action |
|---|---|---|
| **Champions** | Top-performing SKUs driving consistent revenue and new customer acquisition | Protect and amplify — these fund everything else |
| **Growth Drivers** | Products with momentum and scaling potential — high repurchase or strong AOV lift | Invest to accelerate — these become tomorrow's Champions |
| **Underperformers** | SKUs that consume resources (inventory, ad spend, team attention) without return | Reduce exposure, test repositioning, or discontinue |
| **Hidden Gems** | Low-volume SKUs with high unit margin, strong cross-sell potential, or niche audience signal | Protect margin, watch for breakout signals, use in bundles |

---

## Why This Matters: The Product Expansion Error

Expanding the product line is one of the most common ways DTC brands self-sabotage. The failure mode:

1. Champion product is performing well
2. Brand expands to 6–10 additional SKUs
3. Attention and ad spend get diluted across the portfolio
4. OPEX grows to support expanded product management, inventory, and creative
5. Champion product performance degrades without dedicated resources
6. New products don't hit volume needed to justify costs
7. Margins compress; OPEX proportion rises

**The result:** A brand that was profitable at 3 SKUs is unprofitable at 10 SKUs — not because the products are bad, but because the architecture wasn't intentional.

---

## Brand Is LTV, Not Product

Taylor Holiday's frame: "Brand is the lifetime value of your customer."

The proof of a real brand is when customers buy different products, not just repurchase the original. Example: Bambu Earth expected customers to repurchase their flagship skincare product. Instead, customers diversified across the product line. This proved the brand promise was about **trust in solving related problems** — not loyalty to one hero product.

This is the signal that product architecture is working. Customers navigating up and across the portfolio = brand health. Customers only ever buying one SKU = brand fragility.

**30/100 Benchmark:**
- 30% increase in customer value within 60 days = early signal of brand connection
- 100% increase within a year = sustained brand loyalty and product architecture working

## Applying the Framework

**Step 1: Classify every active SKU** — assign to one of the four categories based on:
- Revenue contribution (% of total)
- New customer acquisition rate (does this product drive first purchases?)
- Repeat purchase rate on cohorts acquired through this SKU
- Contribution margin per unit
- Cross-sell rate (does purchasing this lead to other purchases?)

**Step 2: Make allocation decisions by category**
- Champions: protect ad spend allocation first, never defund
- Growth Drivers: assign dedicated creative production and test scaling
- Hidden Gems: use in bundles, email cross-sells, post-purchase flows; do not invest paid media
- Underperformers: set a 90-day decision gate — improve or discontinue

**Step 3: Restrict expansion** — only add new SKUs when you have a clear hypothesis for which category they'll occupy and the resources to build them into that category without cannibalizing Champions.

---

## The SKU Rationalization Trigger

Audit your product architecture when:
- OPEX is rising as a % of revenue
- Champion product ad performance is declining
- AOV is flat despite adding products
- Inventory carrying costs are growing
- Creative production is spread thin across too many products

Any three of these = you have an architecture problem, not a marketing problem.
