# Concept: Generalist-to-Spike Hiring

## Summary / Definition
A two-phase hiring model for scaling companies: hire generalists in the early stage (zero to roughly $10–30M revenue) who can do many things adequately, then transition to hiring "spikes" — people with one or two world-class skills — as the company scales into specialized functions. The timing of this transition is as important as the principle itself. Hiring spikes too early wastes senior talent on work that doesn't yet exist at the required scale; staying with generalists too long creates execution gaps as the business grows.

## Why It Matters
Most hiring advice treats all company stages the same. Early-stage founders hire like they're building a Fortune 500 org chart. Late-stage companies try to retain generalists past their utility. The Generalist-to-Spike model acknowledges that the skills required to get from zero to $10M are fundamentally different from those required to get from $10M to $100M — and that the wrong hire at the wrong time is expensive in both directions.

## How It Works / The Mechanism

### Phase 1: Generalists (0 → ~$10–30M)

At the early stage, the company is too small and too undefined for specialized roles to exist in meaningful form.

A generalist marketer in Phase 1:
- Manages social media
- Runs paid ads
- Shoots content
- Does basic email marketing
- Handles influencer outreach
- Writes copy

They do all of this adequately — not world-class at any one thing, but competent across the board. This is exactly what a 3–5 person company needs. One person doing many things is the only viable operating model at this stage.

**What to look for in Phase 1 generalists:**
- High energy and willingness to do anything
- Adaptability (their job description will change monthly)
- Intrinsic motivation and ownership mentality
- Speed — the early-stage company wins on velocity, not resources
- Hunger over experience

**The experience trap:** Hiring someone experienced from a larger company is often wrong in Phase 1. They're used to having a team of seven supporting their role. At $3M revenue, your "head of sales" is one person. Someone who ran a team at Celsius at $500M joined a fundamentally different operation than the person who was at Celsius from zero to $50M. Always ask: when did they join, and how big was their team?

### Phase 2: Spikes (→ $10M per function, $50M+ company)

As the company scales, functions become large enough to require world-class execution. A generalist who was excellent at Phase 1 may not have the depth required for Phase 2.

Spikes in Phase 2:
- **Marketing spike**: A creative director who is genuinely world-class at brand creative — not also managing email lists
- **Paid media spike**: Someone who runs paid at elite level for brands doing $20M+
- **Supply chain spike**: A demand planner, not a generalist ops person
- **Sales spike**: Someone who has specifically sold a beverage at the right scale (but see the caution below)

**The transition challenge:** Your best Phase 1 generalists may become Phase 2 spikes if they've developed genuine depth in an area. The worst thing you can do is replace loyal Phase 1 people without diagnosing whether they've grown into spikes naturally. Give them the choice of where they want to deepen.

**What to look for in Phase 2 spikes:**
- Deep domain expertise in one or two areas
- Track record at comparable scale — not someone managing a 15-person team when yours is three
- Ability to operate in a resource-constrained environment (they're coming from a bigger company)

### The Interview Question That Catches Phase Mismatch
Ross Mackay's diagnostic: **"When did you join the company?"**

If someone claims Celsius experience, the question isn't whether they worked at Celsius — it's what stage Celsius was at when they joined, and what their team looked like. Joining at $500M is fundamentally different from joining at zero. The patterns of success they learned are different, the resource assumptions are different, the team sizes are different.

A Phase 2 hire from a Phase 3 environment will often try to hire more people, build processes for problems you don't have yet, and struggle with the ambiguity of an early-stage team.

### The "Why Are You Leaving?" Interview Question
Ross's candidate screening question:

*"You're going to get the job today. You start Monday. You call me in three months and say you're leaving. Why?"*

This question is hard to dodge and reveals:
- What the candidate actually needs from a work environment
- Whether their dealbreakers are compatible with the company's current stage
- Whether they're the type to escalate issues or quietly disengage
- Their self-awareness about fit

Answers that reveal Phase mismatch:
- "I want to be involved in areas beyond my defined role" (they need autonomy that may not exist)
- "Work-life balance is very important to me" (mismatched with early-stage pace)
- "I want to influence the brand direction, not just execute" (might not be the execution-focused spike you need)

Answers that reveal strong fit:
- Specific concerns about the work itself, not the environment
- Honest acknowledgment of a gap they'd need to develop
- Focus on impact and outcomes, not role definition

### The Founder as Hiring Architect
Beyond the phase model, the founder's role in hiring is three things:
1. Set the vision
2. Hire the best people in the world (for the current phase)
3. Raise capital if needed

Everything else — including execution, product, operations — is downstream of those three. A founder who is doing the hiring wrong at phase transitions is misallocating their highest-leverage activity.

**Clarity creates velocity**: Help each hire understand exactly how they can move the ball forward toward the company's 2026 or 2030 goal. Ambiguity kills output. The clearer the mandate, the faster the person moves.

### Mentorship as Experience Supplement
Rather than hiring expensive experienced executives who are over-qualified for the current team size, a leaner approach: hire hungry junior spikes and buy experience externally through advisors and consultants on-demand (e.g., Mentor Pass — pay for a one-hour call with someone who's done exactly what you need).

This approach:
- Keeps SG&A lean
- Gives young talent stretch assignments and accelerated development
- Provides pattern-recognition access without full-time compensation burden
- Lets the founder make a bet on hunger rather than resume

## What to Steal / Application

**For founders 0 → $10M:** Hire generalists. Resist the temptation to build a "proper org chart." One good generalist in marketing outperforms two mediocre specialists. Hire for speed, hunger, and adaptability.

**For founders $10M → $50M:** Audit your team. Which generalists have developed genuine depth (natural spikes)? Where are the execution gaps that require a true specialist? Make the transition deliberately, not reactively.

**For anyone hiring:** Ask when they joined previous companies, what their team size was, and what they personally executed versus managed. A resume that says "led marketing at $200M company" tells you almost nothing about what they can do in a 5-person team.
