# Concept: Five Content Games

## Summary
The Five Content Games is a taxonomy of five distinct content-business models, each defined by what's being sold and how trust converts to money. Most content advice fails because it ignores which game the creator is actually playing — strategy that works in Game 2 actively destroys outcomes in Game 5. Before any content decision can be made correctly, the game must be identified first.

---

## The Core Problem This Framework Solves

"Most people will go to a guru or whatever and they'll be like, 'What should my content strategy be?' And most of these gurus don't have the sophistication to be like, 'Well, it depends what your business is. It depends what the offer is.'"

Generic content advice is not wrong — it is context-blind. The five games framework forces context to be established first. Once the game is identified, content strategy, platform selection, ICP targeting, posting frequency, and monetization approach all become derivable.

---

## The Five Games

### Game 1 — Entertainment Media
**Monetization:** CPM (YouTube AdSense, TikTok creator fund), brand deals based on raw view volume
**Goal:** Maximize views at all costs
**Examples:** MrBeast, streamers, viral content accounts
**Definition:** Content you watch without being able to tactically apply anything afterward. You are entertained, but you leave with no new capability or action item.

Entertainment media monetizes attention directly — views equal money, whether through ad revenue or brand deals priced on impressions. This means the only success metric that matters is reach: more eyeballs, more revenue. Content decisions that reduce reach reduce revenue, regardless of quality.

---

### Game 2 — Education Media
**Monetization:** CPM, brand deals (same mechanisms as Game 1)
**Goal:** Maximize views
**Key distinction from Game 1:** "Education videos — you can watch them and then tactically apply something to fix a problem. Anything else is entertainment."

The distinguishing test: can the viewer watch this and immediately do something differently? If yes, it is education. If no — even if it covers a technical topic — it is entertainment.

**The disguised entertainment trap:** Tech commentary, AI news, trend analysis, and many "informational" videos look educational but fail the application test. You are more informed after watching, but you cannot immediately act differently. These are entertainment in educational clothing.

"My tech and AI videos would be like entertainment media. You would think they're education cuz they're like tech, but actually most of my videos you can't walk away and like apply something."

Both Games 1 and 2 are "media games" — they require massive view volume to generate meaningful revenue. CPM rates are low; brand deal rates are moderate; scale is everything.

---

### Game 3 — Commoditized Consumer Product
**Monetization:** Direct product sales
**Product type:** Items people already understand without explanation — clothing, couches, hats, food products people recognize on sight
**Content strategy:** Desire and aesthetics, not education

"Every person can look at a couch and be like that's a couch. So you don't have to educate them on how it works."

The buyer already knows what the product is. The content job is to create desire, demonstrate the product in a compelling context, and establish brand association. A person sitting on the couch saying "yeah I mess with that couch" is a valid content strategy because no additional information is needed — the product communicates itself.

Content for Game 3 optimizes for broad reach and aspirational resonance, not depth or educational value.

---

### Game 4 — Educational Consumer Product
**Monetization:** Direct product sales
**Product type:** Consumer products that require explanation to generate desire
**Example:** AG1 — a green powder that "looks like matcha, could be anything without context." Without knowing what it is, why it's different, and what it does, a viewer has no reason to buy it.

"The viewer needs some amount of education to close the gap. Now they don't need a ton of education. You can give it to them pretty quick, but that's a different content strategy."

Game 4 requires content that closes the understanding gap efficiently — enough education to establish the product's unique value, but not so much that it becomes a long-form course. The content must do both: create desire (Game 3) and build understanding (Games 1/2). This is a harder content challenge than either game alone.

---

### Game 5 — B2B / High-Education Offers
**Monetization:** Business services, coaching programs, info products, B2B SaaS, consulting
**Characteristics:** High trust threshold, long sales cycle, lots of education required before conversion
**Offer types:** Anything where the buyer needs significant context to understand the value proposition and significant trust to take the financial risk

Game 5 is where the Content Minutes framework matters most. Converting someone on a $10,000 coaching program requires ~400 blocks of trust. This means YouTube long-form content is nearly mandatory for Game 5 operators — short-form alone cannot efficiently stack the blocks needed to close high-ticket offers.

Content for Game 5 must demonstrate expertise deeply and repeatedly before conversion is likely. ICP must be narrow by necessity — the offer is specific, and a broad audience contains too many people who are not and will never be the right buyer.

## Education vs. Entertainment: The Definitional Nuance

This distinction is often misunderstood and has large strategic consequences.

**Entertainment:** You watch it, you're engaged, you may be informed, but you leave without a new action you can immediately take.

**Education:** You watch it, and you can do something differently right now because of what you learned.

The subject matter of the content is irrelevant to this classification. A video about cutting-edge AI research is entertainment if you can't do anything with it. A video about a specific Excel formula is education because you can open Excel and use the formula today. This is not a judgment of quality — it determines which game you are playing and therefore which monetization model applies.

## How to Identify Which Game You're Playing

Ask these questions in sequence:

1. **What am I selling?** (Or: What do I want to eventually sell?)
2. **Does the buyer need education to understand the product?**
   - No → Game 3 (commoditized consumer)
   - A little → Game 4 (educational consumer)
   - A lot → Game 5 (B2B/high-education)
3. **Am I selling nothing — just content?**
   - If yes, do viewers leave able to act? → Game 2 (education media)
   - If yes, do viewers just leave entertained? → Game 1 (entertainment media)

The game determines: platform priority, short-form vs. long-form ratio, ICP breadth, content depth, thumbnail strategy, call-to-action type, and success metrics.

## Why the Media Games Are Structurally Declining

Games 1 and 2 (the media games) are under structural threat from two directions simultaneously:

1. **AI content:** AI-generated entertainment and informational content will flood media game formats. The marginal cost of producing media-game content is collapsing toward zero, which means the competitive bar is rising while CPM rates compress.

2. **Incumbent dominance:** "Mr. Beast is one of the winners of that game. Netflix is playing that game. The NFL is playing that game. You will lose to Mr. Beast, Netflix, and the NFL."

A creator starting in 2025/2026 who plays the pure media game is entering a market where the structural forces favor either AI-generated content or billion-dollar incumbents — not independent operators.

**The valid use case for the media game:** As a trust-building bridge. Start in media to build an audience and accumulate blocks, then transition to Games 3, 4, or 5 to actually monetize. The media game as a top-of-funnel mechanism is defensible. The media game as a terminal business model is not.

## Traffic, Funnels, Offers: The Master Hierarchy

All business reduces to three components: Traffic, Funnels, Offers.

- **Traffic:** Getting people to encounter you (content is traffic)
- **Funnels:** The path from encounter to purchase
- **Offers:** What is being sold

Content is not a business — it is a traffic mechanism. The business is the offer. This hierarchy has two starting orientations:

**Offer-first:** You know what you're selling → build content that drives your specific buyer to it → optimize the funnel.

**Traffic-first:** You don't know what you're selling → start making content → build audience → figure out the offer later.

Traffic-first is where most creators end up. It often leads into the media game trap — the traffic is built, but it is too broad to funnel to any specific offer efficiently. The solution is either committing to the media game as a bridge (with a planned offer pivot), or being intentional about ICP narrowing from the start so the traffic you build is pre-qualified for the offer you plan to make.

"All business just boils down to is traffic, funnels, offers. That's it. It's just three things."

---

## What to Steal
Identify your game before making any content decision. If you are in Games 3, 4, or 5, stop optimizing for views — optimize for block accumulation and ICP qualification. If you are in Games 1 or 2, either plan your Game 3/4/5 pivot explicitly or accept the structural ceiling. If your content is "educational" in subject matter but fails the application test, you are actually playing Game 1 — update your strategy accordingly.
