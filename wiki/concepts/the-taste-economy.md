# Concept: The Taste Economy

## Definition
The taste economy describes a cultural and commercial shift in which consumers no longer simply buy what they need at the lowest price — they buy into categories, aesthetics, and quality tiers they previously had no awareness of or access to. Taste has become democratized by the internet and globalized supply chains, creating an economy where having preferences, knowledge, and aesthetic identity is itself a form of social currency.

## The Flavor Analogy
The best entry point to understanding this shift is food. A new generation of engineered flavors — Raising Canes, Sweetgreen, Goop Kitchen — has made dining feel constantly exceptional. Compare this to Doritos and Mountain Dew: hyper-intense artificial flavors that were shocking because nothing had tasted that intense before. Now refined, global, layered flavor is everywhere. The result: plain white bread or a single steamed vegetable now feels like "life with the volume off." Everything around it is that much better.

The non-literal taste economy works the same way. Once a consumer is exposed to genuinely excellent things — whether a Benny rug, an Aesop hand soap, or a rare fragrance — the generic version of the category becomes perceptibly worse by comparison. They can't go back to the commodity. The bar has been permanently raised by exposure.

This is why Uber Eats and premium food delivery thrived — when the "nice thing" is just slightly more than the ordinary thing, the friction of not having it becomes the pain point. The taste economy creates a new baseline.

## Why It Matters in Marketing
In the taste economy, your competitor is not just another brand in the same category — it is an educated consumer who now knows what the best version of your product category looks like. Generic positioning and mid-quality products are increasingly squeezed out. The winning strategy is either to be the clearly best thing, to lead with a compelling ingredient or material story, or to offer a transformation narrative. The enablement ecosystem — creators, influencers, UGC — is the accelerant, not the engine.

## How It Works

### The Two Forces That Created It
1. **Democratized supply chain** — Alibaba, global trade shows, and distributed manufacturing meant the "nice version" of almost anything became accessible and affordable to produce. The price premium for quality collapsed. A genuinely excellent product might cost only marginally more than a mediocre one.
2. **Algorithmic taste exposure** — Social media presents luxury experiences, niche brands, and design excellence in the same feed as everything else. A 14-year-old now knows what a gourmand fragrance is. People understand ingredients, materials, and craftsmanship without ever going looking for that knowledge.

The result: **caring has become easy**. Taste no longer requires effort or access. It arrives at you.

### What the Educated Consumer Looks Like
The modern taste economy consumer:
- Researches before buying ("best X," "worst X," reviews, comparisons) — same habit as before, now expressed across TikTok and Instagram search, not just Google.
- Understands ingredients: skincare actives (niacinamide), textile composition (polyester vs. natural fiber), nutritional science (gut microbiome, probiotics).
- Forms brand relationships through creator education — a creator showing craft, quality, and story converts better than a traditional ad.
- Is enthusiastic — the more educated they get, the more excited they are to participate and spend.

### Three Tactics That Work in the Taste Economy
1. **The Best Thing** — Position as the superlative in your niche. Comfort Hoodies owns "softest hoodie" — thousands of creators making content around this one claim, and every search for "softest hoodie" feeds into it. The story of *how* you became the best (founder quit private equity to make the perfect prebiotic yogurt) performs as content. Algorithms reward search behavior around "best [category]." The search habit hasn't changed — people typed "best foil surfboard" into Google and now type it into TikTok and Instagram. Brands that build around that superlative own that search intent.
2. **The Superpowered Ingredient** — Lead with a specific material, compound, or technique. The Ordinary built a brand by just listing the ingredient on the package (Niacinamide 6%, Zinc 1%) — people Googled it, went down the rabbit hole, and self-educated into brand loyalty. The same pattern applies to creatine, prebiotics/probiotics, natural fiber vs. polyester, gut microbiome products. The ingredient becomes a search attractor and an identity marker.
3. **The Transformation** — Sell the life change, not the product. Link your product to a concrete before/after outcome. This is the emotional close after education. The Peloton, the 30-day program, the espresso machine that makes real craft macchiatos — each one is framed as a life upgrade, not a product purchase.

### The Enthusiasm Loop
The throughline across all three tactics is **enthusiasm**. In the taste economy, the educated consumer is not indifferent — they are excited to participate. They care what they put in their body, they want to know what makes this rug different, they are genuinely thrilled by a rare fragrance.

**Enthusiasm is the byproduct of education.** The more people learn about a category, the more into it they get, the more excited they are to spend. This is the opposite of commoditization. A consumer who doesn't know what a gourmand fragrance is has no preferences. A consumer who does is enthusiastic, willing to spend, and actively recruiting others into the category.

**Fragrance is the canonical example.** Boys aged 14–18 are now obsessed with fragrance. They have full fragrance setups. They learn about notes, samples, layering, and rare finds from TikTok. Fragrance has become part of their identity — a sensory experience that gives them joy and a community. A nine-year-old visiting a shop of rare curiosities in Barcelona or Florence because he's been searching for the most unusual fragrances. This is what education-to-enthusiasm looks like at full intensity.

**Marketing implication:** Build content that educates, and trust that education converts to enthusiasm converts to spending. The creator who explains your product's craft, materials, or science is not just raising awareness — they are converting browsers into enthusiasts. See [[enthusiasm-as-byproduct-of-education]].

### Named Brand Examples
- **Comfort Hoodies** — owns the "softest hoodie" claim; thousands of creators all telling the same superlative story
- **The Ordinary** — ingredient-on-label strategy turned skincare education into brand identity
- **Flamingo Estate** — pandemic-era brand based at a real estate; started with organic vegetable pickup in LA, expanded to hand wash, honey, curated boxes of premium olive oils and strawberries from around the world; sells at a small premium over generic soap; the brand's iconography and story of global curation is the product
- **Benny / Nordic Knots** — rug brands the creator featured in a viral video; Benny makes a "life-changing rug"; the creator/educator is the bridge between unknown quality brand and taste-economy buyer
- **Gruns** — greens supplement that grew through the ingredient (greens) becoming culturally desired
- **ESOP hand soap / Loewe hat / Kate denim** — entry-level luxury items purchased by the Petite Bourgeoisie archetype to signal cultural arrival

### The Four Archetypes of Who Buys Premium/Luxury
Understanding which archetype your customer occupies determines your entire marketing approach:

1. **The Cultural Omnivore** — Educated by experience. Appreciates both a $20 taco truck meal and a Michelin tasting menu equally. Will get the best caviar possible, then still be hungry and get a taco from a truck and enjoy both equally. Buys across classes without hierarchy anxiety. Won't buy entry-level luxury as a signal; buys it because the quality genuinely earns it. Millennial craft movement alumni — this cohort came from the generation that decided the cup pepperoni on pizza matters. Best customer if your product legitimately earns the premium. "A generation raised on Bourdain."

2. **The Petite Bourgeoisie** — Consuming above their means to signal arrival. Reads the right substacks (Man Repeller, The Strategist), watches the influencer content, follows what creators are telling them to consume. Buys ESOP hand soap, a Loewe hat, Kate denim — all entry-level luxury purchases they arguably shouldn't be making yet. They are "spending above the money they have" to signal cultural arrival. **Marketing approach for this archetype:** give them an on-ramp, focus on making them feel they've entered the brand journey. What do they get? What recognition or experience awaits them? How do you begin building them toward greater loyalty over time?

3. **The True Elite** — Wealth so embedded that buying a $3,000 coat is just buying a coat. Unreachable through social media ads — they do not follow luxury brands on Instagram (aspiring consumers do that). The True Elite luxury consumer has never seen a Gucci AI ad campaign. **How to reach them:** long-form documentary, live in-person experiences (boat shows, country clubs, luxury demo days), generational brand reputation. Example: a foiling brand that reached this consumer through a long-form documentary featuring Laird Hamilton about foiling and NASA — shared through the networks of people who "spend their time, hobbies, and money doing intense things around the world." Building for this customer is a multi-decade project. "Your kids are going to finish it off."

4. **The Aspiring** — Not yet educated; buys purely on logo signal. Sees the Gucci logo and wants it. Will buy the cheapest available item purely as a status signal, whether or not they have the money. The risk/reward tension for luxury brands: too many aspirational buyers erodes perceived luxury; too few means missing an entire generation who may become omnivores over time. Entry-point products are their on-ramp.

**The practical prescription:** If you sell a premium good, zone in on one or two of these archetypes at a time. Think through: what products do you have for each, what marketing channel reaches each, and what is the customer journey from entry-point to deeper brand loyalty?

### The Enabled Economy
The taste economy requires a marketing stack where organic brand content, enabled accounts (gifted creators, seeded influencers), paid amplification, and live experiences all reinforce each other. The old marketing stack was: owned social accounts + paid ads (Meta, banners, billboards, magazines) + a celebrity endorsement or two. The new stack adds the **enabled tier** — tens, hundreds, or thousands of creators seeded with product or experiences who make content organically. Then paid media amplifies what's working in organic.

**The formula: Organic + Enabled + Paid Amplification = a far larger share of cultural conversation than any of the three alone.**

The Gap's Coachella booth is the definitive example: they spent approximately $1M on an activation selling Coachella-branded hoodies. The question isn't whether they recouped the cost in hoodie sales (they probably didn't). The question is what they bought: access to Coachella, the most-documented event of the year, attended by tens of thousands of people who overindex on posting on social media. Every person in line for the hoodie makes a video. Everyone who buys it wears it in Coachella content. Coachella content significantly outperforms regular content in engagement. The brand bought an **enabling ripple effect** — far more valuable than equivalent media spend elsewhere.

**The billboard principle extends this:** A Times Square billboard is no longer valued for how many people see it — it's valued for how many people photograph it and share it. The impression is the photo, not the eyes. Every physical presence in culture now gets evaluated through this lens.

## Key Takeaways
- Taste is no longer innate or class-gated; it is algorithmically delivered and socially rewarded.
- The educated consumer will find a better competitor unless you tell them, clearly, why you are the best option.
- Three content angles outperform everything: the best thing, the key ingredient, the transformation.
- Know which archetype your customer is — it determines your price point, your creative tone, and your marketing channel mix.
- The enablement layer (creators, influencers, events) is not optional — in the taste economy, third-party voices are how taste gets transferred and validated.
- Education produces enthusiasm, not just knowledge. Enthusiastic consumers spend more, recruit others, and build category identity.
- The True Elite cannot be reached digitally — they require long-form storytelling, live experiences, and generational brand building.
- The flavor analogy: once a consumer has tasted genuinely excellent things, the generic version feels like "life with the volume off." You can't un-educate a taste economy consumer.
