# Strategy: Building Creative Collectives

## What This Is For

This strategy is for any creative — designer, brand builder, artist, director, content creator, photographer — who wants to operate inside a network of collaborators that amplifies their individual talent rather than in isolation. It applies whether you are at the beginning of your career, trying to find your first serious collaborators, or more established and trying to build the kind of creative ecosystem that sustains long-term output.

The Been Trill / Donda orbit produced Matthew Williams (Alyx, later CD at Givenchy), Heron Preston, Justin Saunders (Jound), Tremaine Emory (Denim Tears, Supreme CD), and Virgil Abloh. Supreme built and maintained a living collective of skaters, photographers, musicians, and directors over 30 years that drove $500+ million in annual revenue. These are not exceptions — they are the model.

---

## The Framework

**Phase 1: Find the Room**
Identify the specific physical spaces, projects, or institutions that are operating at the level you want to reach. Get into proximity with the people who are already there.

**Phase 2: Qualify on Obsession**
Filter collaborators not by credentials or follower counts but by obsession level. The collective forms between people who are completely consumed by the same things.

**Phase 3: Find the Gravitational Center**
Every productive collective has an anchor — a larger project, institution, creative director, or movement that gives the collective a shared purpose and a reason to be in the same room.

**Phase 4: Enable the First Breakout**
The collective doesn't need everyone to rise simultaneously. It needs one person to break through first — to open doors, establish credibility, and create an audience that others can reach.

**Phase 5: Formalize the Sharing**
Build explicit structures for sharing references, work in progress, feedback, and opportunities. The default online behavior is broadcasting finished work; the collective norm should be sharing unfinished thinking.

**Phase 6: Tend the Relationships**
The long-term collective is built on maintained relationships. This requires active, ongoing investment — not just in times of collaboration but continuously.

## The Details

### Finding Collaborators

**The obsession filter is everything.** "Find people who are on that same skill level or obsession level with you or one level higher and really take your relationships with them seriously." The criterion is not who is most talented by external measure, but who is living with the same level of consuming preoccupation with the work. "I'm truly obsessed with everything. I'm obsessed with all social media, with all design. All I want to do 24/7 is create things, think about how to master these things." That level of engagement is the self-selection mechanism for a real collective.

**Local is still primary.** "If creatives start really leveraging the internet to get together in their cities to put other people on to work together to form brands and coexist between brands in an interesting way." The Donda/Been Trill orbit was physically located in Chicago and New York. The Supreme ecosystem was physically located in lower Manhattan and then Fairfax in LA. Digital connection maintains relationships; physical proximity creates them.

**Bring the internet into real life.** "Send more DMs, bring more internet into real life, connect with more people." The internet surfaces who the people are that you should be meeting. The actual meeting needs to happen in person.

**Look one level up.** Find people who are one level of skill or experience above where you are currently. The collective functions as a training environment — proximity to people slightly further ahead accelerates your own development.

### Structuring the Dynamic

**The collective is not a company.** Do not formalize it prematurely. The productive creative energy of a collective comes from its looseness — the ability to work together when it's useful, to work independently when it isn't, and to maintain relationships across both modes. The Been Trill collective was a set of relationships, not an LLC.

**Share credit explicitly and early.** One of the fastest ways to fracture a collective is credit ambiguity. When you collaborate, name your collaborators publicly. Credit is not zero-sum — acknowledging others costs you nothing and builds the trust that makes future collaboration possible.

**Have a gravitational center outside yourselves.** The most durable collectives orbit something larger than any individual member — a brand, a movement, an institution, or a cultural moment. The Donda orbit had Kanye's work as its center. Supreme's orbit had the brand itself. Without this shared gravity, collectives dissolve when the next interesting project pulls members in different directions.

**Establish explicit sharing norms.** "There's almost a hesitation to share just how obsessed you are" online. Inside the collective, that hesitation should not exist. Create the explicit norm that you share work-in-progress, references, obsessions, and half-baked ideas freely. The collective is the context where you can be completely honest about what you're working on and thinking about.

### Maintaining the People Network at Scale

Supreme's model required an extraordinary operational investment. "The amount of juggling that it takes to get this many skaters, this many directors, this many lookbooks, this many rappers, this many brand collabs requires so much interpersonal relationships, so much hunting stuff down and texting and DMing and calling and meeting up and keeping those relationships over time." This is not glamorous. It is relationship management at scale.

Practical elements of this:
- Maintain contact with everyone in your network, not just when you need something.
- Track what people are working on and where they are in their careers.
- Introduce people to each other across your network — "putting other people on" is one of the most valuable things you can do for the collective as a whole.
- Keep records of collaborators, conversations, and commitments. "I imagine they had to use a CRM at one point to be able to manage all the brand deals, the skaters, the photographers, then the personalities."

### Enabling the First Breakout

The collective needs someone to break through first. That person's rise should function as a lift for the entire network, not just for themselves. When Virgil became CD at Louis Vuitton, he "was bringing in every creative that he was excited about working with, leveraging all of his connections and ideas." The breakout member uses their platform to create opportunities for the rest of the collective — that is what makes the collective worth belonging to.

Practically: when you are the person who breaks through, bring people with you. When someone else in your network breaks through, make yourself available and useful to them. The collective is maintained by reciprocity, and the reciprocity needs to survive the status differential that comes when one member rises faster than others.

### The People Curation Skill

Supreme's core competency was not design — it was people curation. "What I want to call out about these people is that they not only propelled skate culture continually into the mainstream... but they also supported it fiscally in a significant way." The $1 million/year payment to Tyshawn Jones was not sponsorship — it was an investment in the culture that the brand depended on.

For any brand or creative entity that wants to build a collective: **your approach to people is your differentiation.** "What is your people ops? Not just seeding free stuff to get people to wear it. How are you building your version of the Stüssy tribe or what Supreme did here inside your own region or your own subcultures in a really interesting way?"

The question to answer: in your category, your city, your niche — who are the people that matter? Who are the skaters, the photographers, the musicians, the directors? What does it cost to invest in them meaningfully? What do you get back?

## Common Mistakes

**Formalizing too early.** Converting a creative orbit into a company before the relationships are tested and the complementary skills are clear produces equity disputes and creative friction without the benefit of institutional stability.

**Building online-only collectives.** Digital connection is not collective formation. It is information sharing. Without physical presence and proximity, the osmosis that makes collectives productive does not happen.

**Filtering on credentials rather than obsession.** The person with the impressive resume and the casual attitude toward the work will not sustain the collective. The person with fewer credentials and complete obsession will.

**Asymmetric investment.** When one member is doing the work of maintaining relationships while others coast on the collective's social capital, the collective fractures. The obligation is symmetric: everyone maintains, everyone brings opportunities, everyone shares.

**Treating the collective as a closed system.** The healthiest collectives are porous — they allow new people in and let members graduate out without treating either as a betrayal. The Been Trill members became individual brands; that was the correct outcome of the incubator working.

**Confusing the collective with the work.** The collective exists to serve the work, not the other way around. When the collective becomes its own purpose — when maintaining the group dynamic takes precedence over producing — it has outlived its function.

## What Success Looks Like

In the short term (1-3 years): You have 3-7 people you can call at 2am about something you're working on and receive a useful, honest response. You have made at least one collaboration that you could not have made alone. You have introduced people in your network to each other who then worked together without you.

In the medium term (3-7 years): When one person in the collective rises, they bring opportunities to others. The collective's credibility compounds — being associated with it carries weight because the people in it are known. Individual members develop distinct identities that are complementary rather than competitive.

In the long term (7+ years): Some members have graduated to their own significant projects. The relationships formed in the collective continue to produce collaboration, referral, and support across different institutional contexts. The collective has produced something — a cultural moment, a body of work, a generation of brands — that could not have been produced by any individual member alone.
