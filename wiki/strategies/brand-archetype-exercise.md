# Strategy: Brand Archetype Exercise

## Summary
A structured workshop methodology for choosing the right brand social media archetype. Translates the abstract question "what should we post?" into a resource-reality decision that leadership can approve and a team can execute. Used by Cut 30 across hundreds of brands from startups to Fortune 500s. Works as a standalone client deliverable, a pitch-to-leadership document, or an internal strategy session.

## When to Use This
- Before building a content calendar or choosing content pillars
- When a brand has been posting but nothing is working
- When a freelancer or agency needs structured buy-in from a client
- When an in-house team needs to present a content strategy to leadership
- When a brand is starting social media from scratch and needs a north star

## The Problem This Solves
Most brands have a vision for amazing content — and rarely execute it. The gap between vision and execution is almost always resources: not enough people, not enough budget, not the right talent available. Most content strategy sessions skip resource mapping and produce a wish-list plan that dies in implementation. The archetype exercise forces resource reality first, so the strategy that comes out is one the org can actually run.

---

## The Exercise: Step-by-Step

### Step 1 — Resource Audit
Before looking at archetypes, map what the organization actually has or can realistically get:

**People:**
- Who can be on camera for the brand? (Founders, staff, contractors, hireable creators)
- Is there a genuine expert in the org? Or someone who can play the student-expert role?
- Is there someone entertaining, charismatic, or funny on staff?
- Are there multiple team members who could rotate on camera?

**Design / Production:**
- Who can design or edit for the brand? (Internal designer, agency already retained, budget to hire one)
- What production resources exist? (Camera, location, studio, editing capability)
- Is the org willing to hire a creator to run the brand account?

**Content Material:**
- What's actually happening at the company that could generate content?
- What do customers want to understand about the brand's world?
- What's interesting about how the product is made, sourced, or used?
- Are there industry events, trade shows, or behind-the-scenes moments available?

**Commitment Level:**
- What is leadership willing to fund? (Talent, production, ad spend)
- What is the realistic posting frequency?
- How much risk is the org willing to take? (Standard playbook vs. creative moonshot)

---

### Step 2 — Cross-Reference Resources with Archetypes

Walk through each archetype and test it against the resource audit:

| Archetype | Core Resource Required |
|-----------|----------------------|
| The Oracle | A credible expert (founder, staff specialist, or hireable spokesperson) who can storytell about the category |
| The Performer | Someone genuinely funny, charismatic, or entertaining — on staff or available to hire |
| The World Builder | Creative talent willing to take risk; budget for higher production; org appetite for a moonshot |
| The Catalyst | Content about the brand's world that pushes the audience toward a better version of themselves; no single deep expert required |
| The Helper | A relatable person who's one step ahead of the audience; multiple creators work; no expert required |

If the org has a genuine expert → Oracle is the no-brainer choice.
If the org has entertaining personalities → Performer.
If nothing has worked and there's budget → World Builder (the moonshot).
If neither expert nor performer is available → Catalyst or Helper, depending on whether the brand's content should inspire (Catalyst) or guide (Helper).

---

### Step 3 — Pick One Archetype
Commit to one archetype. Not two. Not "we'll try Oracle on weekdays and Performer on weekends." One north star.

The common failure mode: brands try to be everything to everybody on social. Archetypes solve this by forcing a single consistent persona. Audiences can't follow a brand that doesn't have a coherent identity.

Blending happens naturally over time as the brand matures. Start with one. Execute it until it works.

---

### Step 4 — Brainstorm Content Ideas Within the Archetype
Once the archetype is chosen, generate a specific brainstorm list of 15–30 content ideas within that archetype's parameters. This is the deliverable that creates buy-in.

The list should be concrete:
- Not "educational content about our category" → "the history of why suits became formal wear"
- Not "helpful tips for customers" → "which Korean skincare serums are actually worth it for oily skin"
- Not "entertaining brand content" → "a recurring Office-style skit set in our warehouse with these three characters"

When the team sees a list of ideas they're already excited about, the strategy has buy-in before the first post goes live.

---

### Step 5 — Connect to Content Pillars and Ongoing Execution
Take the archetype + brainstorm list and connect it to a content pillar system for ongoing production. The archetype is the persona; the content pillars are the topic categories that the archetype covers repeatedly over time.

The output of the full exercise:
1. Resource map (what the org has)
2. Chosen archetype (with rationale)
3. Brainstorm list of 15–30 specific ideas
4. Draft content pillars (3–5 topic areas the archetype will own)
5. Posting cadence and format decisions

This is a full content strategy document that can be presented to a client or to leadership in a single meeting.

---

## Why This Works for Leadership Buy-In

Archetypes are not a social media native concept — they come from traditional brand strategy, which CMOs and executives already know. Presenting a social media strategy in archetype language speaks their language:

- **Old pitch:** "We should post more reels and try trending audios."
- **Archetype pitch:** "We recommend the Oracle archetype — positioning the brand as the definitive expert in [category]. Here's the resource map showing we have [expert name] available, here are 20 content ideas the team is excited about, and here are the content pillars we'll own."

The second pitch gets approved. It answers the strategic question before the tactical one.

For a younger in-house employee pitching up, the archetype exercise provides a structure that feels credible to senior stakeholders — not just another content calendar, but a strategic persona decision backed by resource reality.

---

## Using This as a Freelancer / Agency Deliverable
Running the archetype exercise with a client produces several outputs that strengthen the engagement:

1. **Discovery conversation** — the resource audit becomes a structured client interview that uncovers what the org can actually do
2. **Strategy document** — the archetype + brainstorm list becomes a deliverable that justifies the strategy engagement fee
3. **Proposal anchor** — the resource map drives the scope of work (who they need to hire, what production budget is required, what the freelancer will vs. won't do)
4. **Accountability framework** — when the client later wants to drift from the archetype, you have a document to point back to

The rarest skill set in social media is not knowing trends or running ads — it's being able to run this process, generate a strategy the org can execute, and then make it actually work.
