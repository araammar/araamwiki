# Strategy: Newsletter Writing Framework (Harry Dry Method)
tags: [newsletter, writing, email, content-strategy, engagement, copywriting]
applies_to: [newsletter writers, content creators, brand newsletters, founder newsletters]

## Summary

Harry Dry's approach to writing his Marketing Examples newsletter — 100K+ subscribers, with engagement rates no other newsletter matches. The framework covers how to think about newsletters differently, how to open each edition, how to structure the content, and the writing philosophy that makes people actually read and reply.

## The Reframe: Letter, Not News

> "It's less about the news and more about the letter." — Hanley

A newsletter isn't a blog post emailed to people. It's a letter from one person to another. That distinction changes everything about how you write it.

A blog post can be impersonal, informational, reference-material. A letter has a person on the other side. It has time, place, energy, doubt. When Harry writes, he starts with "where am I and what's going on right now?" — not "what information do I have to share?"

## Framework / Steps

### Step 1 — Open With Time, Place, and What's Going On

Every Harry newsletter starts with a brief, specific scene. Not introductory preamble — a moment.

JK Rowling opened Chamber of Secrets: "Not for the first time, an argument had broken out over breakfast at Number Four, Privet Drive." In one sentence: time (breakfast), place (Number Four), what's happening (argument). You're in the scene immediately.

Harry's newsletter version: "Live from London. Weather update: trees shuttering. Majestic hail."

He looked out the window. It wrote itself. The reader can't get this from Twitter. It happened to one person at one moment. That specificity is what makes it feel like a letter.

Another example: "Hey, it's Harry. It's 3:47 a.m. I'm in the big smoke alone. Laptop, green tea, looking at the whiteboard like a dozy dog. Six words on it. Five with lines through. Intro glares at me. I glare back. The final horcrux. Sip some tea. Is this any good? I don't know. My tea tastes cold. I should press send."

That intro tells you: time (3:47 a.m.), place (London office), what's happening (writing the newsletter, five of six sections done, struggling with the intro). It's not clever — it's real. And real is what makes people feel like they're reading a letter from someone who matters.

### Step 2 — Dense Examples (5–6 per Issue)

Most newsletters are either too long (blog post stretched to email) or too thin (three bullets and a link). Harry's are deliberately dense: 5–6 complete examples per issue, each self-contained.

The density serves two functions:
1. High-information-per-minute ratio — readers feel rewarded for their time
2. Each example is a standalone unit that can be shared, screenshotted, or referenced independently

### Step 3 — Conflict in Every Example

> "We remember things relatively."

The pickle juice principle: give someone pickle juice, then orange juice — the orange juice tastes sweeter by contrast. Give them orange juice alone — it's just orange juice. To make your insight land, you need the contrast that sets it up.

Every example in Harry's newsletter has some form of conflict:
- Before / after
- Here's the problem / here's the solution
- Here's how everyone else does it / here's how they do it differently

The Loom example: Harry doesn't open with "Loom's positioning is great." He opens with "here's how every other screen recorder positions their product" — then shows how Loom went from "screen recorder" to "async video messaging" because "remote communication sucks." The contrast is the teaching.

Without the before, there is no after. Write the setup, then the reveal.

### Step 4 — No Paragraph Longer Than Two Lines

Harry hasn't written a paragraph longer than two lines since March 2021.

Three reasons:
1. Short paragraphs are easy to swing between (monkey butts). People read on trains, in distractions.
2. If a paragraph runs to three lines, he's usually explaining himself poorly — cutting it forces clarity
3. Lines need room to breathe — good sentences shouldn't be crowded

This isn't a style affectation. It's a reading-conditions decision. Your newsletter competes with everything else the reader has open. Make it easy to read fast.

### Step 5 — Write in the Medium

Never write your newsletter in Google Docs and paste it in. Write it directly in your email tool (ConvertKit, Substack, Beehiiv — wherever it will actually appear).

Why: line breaks, font sizes, and column widths all affect how copy reads. A sentence that works in a Word doc might spill across three uncomfortable lines in your email template. You need to see it as the reader will see it.

Harry: "I don't trust when I drag it in — a line might spill to three lines or be four lines. I don't like that. I want to see it just as the reader reads it."

This applies to all copy: write ads in Figma, newsletters in the newsletter tool, billboards placed among other billboards.

## When to Use

- Launching or improving a newsletter with an editorial voice
- Writing any long-form content that will be read at pace (on a phone, in transit)
- Structuring a weekly or monthly roundup with examples and analysis
- Building an email brand that people actually look forward to receiving

## Examples

**The newsletter intro template:**
> "Hey, it's [name]. It's [time]. I'm in [place]. [One specific observable detail about the moment.] [What's happening or what's on my mind.] Here's what I've got for you."

**Conflict structure for each example:**
> "[Here's how everyone else approaches this.] // [Here's what [Company] did differently.] // [Here's what changed as a result.]"
