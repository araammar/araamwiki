# Strategy: Marketing Campaign Masterclass

## Summary
A complete framework for planning and executing a marketing campaign — from the strategic anchor (insight or concept) through channel tiers, content phases, and world-building with partners, retailers, and customers. The standard for a well-executed campaign is 15–22 pieces of content output from a single launch, with all channels firing simultaneously to create a compounding attention effect.

## When to Use This
- Launching a new product, feature, or seasonal collection
- Planning a campaign that needs to reach beyond your existing follower base
- When you want to justify a shoot or production investment by maximizing asset output
- When coordinating influencers, retailers, and internal content teams in parallel

## The Framework / Steps

### Step 1: Define the Strategic Anchor — Insight or Concept
Every campaign begins with one of two starting points:
- **Insight:** A discovered truth about your customer — a habit, frustration, or desire you can capitalize on. Example: people know they need greens, but the current experience is terrible. A gummy format solves it.
- **Concept:** A visual or narrative presentation idea, especially useful when you lack a strong insight. The Patrick Ta "The Menu" campaign is concept-first — food preparation as the metaphor for the makeup launch.

You need at least one. When you have both, your campaign will be significantly stronger. The insight determines *what* you say; the concept determines *how* it looks and feels.

### Step 2: Map Your Channel Tiers
Identify every channel you'll use and assign it to a tier:

**Tier 1 — Core (owned channels):**
Your brand social accounts, email list, SMS list, website, any owned ad accounts, founder or team member accounts. These are your baseline. A small brand's core may be a single Instagram account; a larger brand has a full stack.

**Tier 2 — Extensions:**
Retail partners (co-posts, newsletter features, in-store signage), long-term influencer partners, co-promotion deals with adjacent brands or software integrations, pop-up events inside retailer locations.

**Tier 3 — Partners and Customers:**
Existing customers can be activated as promoters — offer them early access, customer-only pricing, or one-on-one outreach before launch. Customers are often the most overlooked lever. Also: merchandising — store gondolas, pallet wrap design, digital signage at point of sale.

### Step 3: Plan the Content Phases (15–22 Pieces Target)
A well-built campaign produces 15–22 pieces of content. Pre-plan every piece before the shoot — you will never extract maximum assets from a shoot without a shot list tied to these phases.

**Phase 1 — Teaser**
1–3 posts. Low-stakes. Creates anticipation. Can be a cryptic single image, a chyron carousel ("crash is coming"), or conceptual foreshadowing. If it performs, great. If not, don't over-invest.

**Phase 2 — Unveil**
1–3 posts. First reveal of the product or campaign concept. Not the full launch — just the moment of discovery. Half-teaser, half-reveal is fine. The goal is "oh, this is what's happening."

**Phase 3 — Launch**
The flagship content. Your main video or visual campaign asset. Shoot multiple angles and variations — never bet everything on one piece. Re-cut the same footage into a meme carousel, a wide-angle version, and an FPV version. Each variation is a distinct post.

**Phase 4 — Extend**
Tutorial content, unboxing walkthroughs, FAQ-style videos answering top customer questions. These drive less reach but are high bottom-of-funnel value — they help people who are already interested actually convert. This works identically for SaaS (feature walkthrough, split-screen demo answering comments) as it does for physical products.

**Phase 5 — Performance**
Ad-specific assets. Separate from organic. Standard ad checklist: problem-solution ad, founder story ad, selfish desire ad, reply ad (responding to a comment), magazine editorial style, testimonial static. Build this checklist before the shoot so you capture the necessary footage.

**Phase 6 — Refresh**
Two weeks to two months post-launch, plan a second wave. Brief UGC creators on a new concept or insight tied to the same product. Respond to top comments and engineer follow-up content from them. Pre-plan this at the time of the original launch so execution doesn't stall.

### Step 4: Build the Brand World
Your campaign lives beyond your own channels. Map who else will be posting, and coordinate timing so everything fires as close together as possible.

**Influence:** Pro athletes, sponsored creators, micro-influencers briefed on a unified concept (like Gel Blaster's video game scenario brief). Even a micro with 10K followers executing a great concept can drive millions of views.

**Retailers:** If you're in retail, leverage their newsletter, their social accounts, and their in-store formats. Being better at social than your retailers gives you leverage — they want collab posts too.

**Seeding:** PR kits, product sends, free trial access for SaaS. Design the kit intentionally. Document unboxings as part of the content plan.

**Events:** Piggybacking on a trade show, hosting a VIP Zoom, or doing a customer pop-up at HQ are all valid event strategies at any budget. HubSpot's Inbound event is the enterprise version; a 40-person customer dinner is the startup version. Both work.

**Crossover:** Organic posts that perform become ads. Influencer posts get spark-ad spend. Partner posts go into email. The 1+1+1+1=6.5 principle: simultaneous deployment across channels creates compounding attention that exceeds the sum of individual parts.

## Real Examples
- **Break Archive Black Friday:** 15 posts total — teaser chyrons, hot girl investing carousel as the main launch concept, meme carousels for shareability, multiple promotional posts. Classic well-executed campaign.
- **Patrick Ta "The Menu":** Conceptual campaign with food/cooking theme. Teaser apron reveal → partial unveil → coming soon post → 24-hour early access → full product showcase. Art direction-forward with no single "big video" moment.
- **Tier Mavericks Shoe Launch:** 22 content pieces from two filming locations, built around a mad scientist narrative. Drone footage, FPV reel, lab set carousels, athlete footage, multiple recuts. Planned entirely in advance.
- **Gel Blaster at Costco:** Briefed micro-influencers on a video game scenario concept to support a Costco-exclusive bundled product. Brief smashed with millions of views from a creator with under 50K followers.
- **Lift Foils:** Campaign world built through hundreds of reseller/rental affiliates. Fed them content, collab posted with the best performers, ran regional paid spend behind their content. Brand world multiplied by the affiliate network.
- **HubSpot Inbound:** Anchored a Loop Marketing launch to a trade show event. Deployed through owned social, sponsored creator network, podcast network, and advertising simultaneously — B2B version of the same playbook.

## Pitfalls to Avoid
- **Shooting without a content plan:** You will come out with 2 assets from a shoot you could have turned into 22. Pre-plan every phase, angle, and format before the shoot starts.
- **Betting on one big video:** The social algorithm era rewards multiple shots at the same concept. Recut, reframe, restructure into formats (carousels, memes, FAQs).
- **Skipping the refresh:** Launch traction fades in 2–8 weeks. Without a pre-planned wave two, campaigns die instead of compound.
- **Not activating customers:** Your most loyal buyers are an untapped promotional layer. Early access, customer-only pricing, and personal outreach convert them into advocates.
- **Standardized budgets:** If the economics are working, push for more volume. A fixed marketing budget is a ceiling — if the product and ROI justify it, scale spend with results.
- **Working with production firms that don't think in content phases:** If your production partner doesn't understand the teaser-to-refresh arc, they will under-deliver assets.
