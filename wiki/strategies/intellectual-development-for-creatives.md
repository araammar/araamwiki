# Strategy: Intellectual Development for Creatives

## What This Is For

This is a structured approach to building a mental model library — the internal framework of knowledge, perspectives, and ways of seeing that determines the quality of your creative decisions. It is for any creative who has recognized that their work is limited not by their technical skills but by the originality and depth of their thinking. It applies to designers, brand builders, writers, directors, strategists, and anyone else whose output depends more on their perspective than on any specific technical capability.

The core problem this addresses: "We all scroll these same algorithms... no matter how nuanced or interesting we think we are, we're being shown a lot of the same content." The intellectual development strategy is the deliberate counter-program to algorithmic convergence.

---

## The Framework

**Level 1: Establish a sustainable reading practice**
Consistency over volume. A sustainable practice you can maintain indefinitely produces more than an intensive burst you abandon.

**Level 2: Build a diverse reading diet by category**
Read across categories — design, oral history, fiction, business, craft, mindset, politics — to build a mental model library that spans disciplines. The breadth is what produces genuinely original synthesis.

**Level 3: Apply what you read to active problems**
Reading without application produces information, not mental models. Connect each book to a specific decision, problem, or context in your current work.

**Level 4: Build a personal reference library**
Not just a reading list — a physical or organized digital library of things you can return to. The reference library is a long-term asset.

**Level 5: Cultivate contrarian reading**
Deliberately read material that challenges your existing positions — not to be persuaded, but to understand how strong arguments are constructed and to sharpen your own critical thinking.

## The Details

### Level 1: The Reading Practice

**The baseline recommendation:** 30 minutes before sleep, off all screens, every night. This produces approximately one book per month, occasionally two. Over five years, that is 60-120 books — a genuinely substantial mental model library if chosen well.

The comparison point: "I used to read a book a week when I was younger, pre-social media." The decline in reading rate is not about having less time — it is about having more screen competition for the same time. The practice of going screen-free for 30 minutes before sleep is not primarily about sleep hygiene; it is about protecting reading time from the default of screen consumption.

**For dense, foundational books:** Don't read linearly. "Literally skip through this book. Open up any section of a page and you're going to find some really interesting nugget." Dense books like Capslock are reference volumes, not narrative reads. Pick them up, find the relevant section for a current problem, read that section, and put them down. Return when the next problem surfaces.

**For oral histories and narratives:** These read linearly and quickly. Give them full attention. The oral history format — 50+ people telling the same story from their own perspectives — produces a kind of understanding that synthesized non-fiction cannot. You get the contradictions, the gaps, and the texture of a real human situation.

**For fiction:** Read it like fiction. Do not instrumentalize it by reading for lessons. Read Blood Meridian to be inside the world Cormac McCarthy creates. The application to creative work comes later, from having had the experience of being inside an immersive world rather than from extracting lessons while you read.

### Level 2: The Reading Diet by Category

The categories are not equally weighted. Adjust based on where your specific gaps are. The key is that no category is absent.

**Design and the Built World**
The priority read here is Capslock — "probably the most impactful book I've read in terms of the lens with which I view the world." Secondary: Emotion by Design (Nike CMO, for understanding how large-scale brand thinking works), Like Art by Glenn O'Brien (for understanding advertising as a cultural practice with its own absurdity and intelligence).

What these build: a habit of seeing designed objects and visual environments as intentional — as expressions of choices that could have been made differently, that serve specific interests, that reflect specific moments in the history of how human beings have shaped experience through design.

**Context and Oral History**
Priority reads: Powerhouse (oral history of CAA) and These Guys Have All the Fun (oral history of ESPN). Secondary: The Best of Grantland anthologies.

What these build: temporal perspective. Most creatives operate in a cultural present that feels unique to them — unprecedented, without obvious historical analogs. Oral histories collapse that feeling. The forces that disrupted Hollywood talent management in the CAA era are the same forces disrupting creator economics now. The forces that made ESPN dominant are the same forces that destroyed it. Having the first iteration in mind makes the current iteration legible.

**Mindset and Resistance**
Priority reads: The War of Art by Steven Pressfield and The Obstacle Is the Way by Ryan Holiday. Secondary: The Courage to Be Disliked, Meaning at Work.

What these build: a vocabulary for the internal obstacles that prevent creative output. The War of Art names the enemy (the Resistance). The Obstacle Is the Way gives the operational principle (the thing you are most avoiding is the thing you most need to do). Without this framework, the experience of creative avoidance feels personal and shameful — as if it reflects a character flaw rather than a universal human tendency that has a structure and can therefore be countered.

Read The War of Art alongside Gates of Fire by the same author. The juxtaposition — self-help book and historical novel, both by someone who dealt deeply with creative avoidance while producing genuinely great work — makes the advice credible in a way that most self-help books are not.

**Business and Operations**
Priority reads: $100 Million Offers by Alex Hormozi (for structuring what you sell), Made to Stick by Chip and Dan Heath (for communicating what you make), Becoming Trader Joe's (for understanding immaculate brand positioning in practice). Secondary: Super Pumped (for the aggressive end of brand building), EOS/Traction (for when you have more than 10 people to manage).

What these build: a realistic understanding of the structural requirements of turning creative vision into sustainable enterprise. Most creatives underinvest in business and operations reading. The result is a persistent gap between the quality of what they make and their ability to build lasting value around it. "$100 Million Offers changed my life, will probably change yours" — the specific value is teaching creatives how to structure and package their services into something that can be sold repeatedly rather than sold as an undifferentiated time resource.

**Craft and Technical Vocabulary**
Priority reads: Atlas of Modern Clothing (garment nomenclature) and Textileedia (fabric knowledge). Secondary: House of Errors (brand building arc, realistic version), Sponsor Magnet (creator economy mechanics).

What these build: the specific technical vocabulary of your discipline. For fashion: the ability to communicate precisely with manufacturers and designers rather than "sending mood board picks and hoping for the best." The Atlas of Modern Clothing teaches garment nomenclature; Textileedia teaches materials. Together they bridge the gap between aesthetic vision and technical specification.

**Fiction and World-Building**
Priority reads: Blood Meridian by Cormac McCarthy (for world-building through immersive prose). Secondary: Zerville, The Adderall Diaries (contemporary American voice), The Three-Body Problem by Liu Cixin (non-Western narrative structure).

What these build: the experience of being inside a world that someone else constructed through language and craft. This is not separable from the ability to build a world through your own creative work. "You begin to notice how easily someone does that with words and story. I think it's really impactful to think about how you can apply those same principles to building your world." The Three-Body Problem is specifically for anyone whose creative work intersects with Asian markets or audiences — it demonstrates, more efficiently than any explanation, how differently people in different parts of the world structure heroism, collective action, and narrative.

### Level 3: Applying What You Read to Active Problems

The test of a mental model is whether you reach for it when you encounter a relevant problem. The reading builds the library; the application installs the mental model.

**During reading:** When you encounter an idea that connects to something in your current work, stop and write the connection down. Don't wait until the end of the book.

**After reading:** For every significant book, identify one specific decision or problem in your current work that the book's framework addresses. Apply it explicitly. The $100 Million Offers offer-structuring framework should be applied to your actual service offerings, not treated as abstract learning.

**In conversation:** Share what you're reading with the people in your collective. "The more interesting and wide ranging that is, the more excited you're going to be and the more interesting work you're going to do." The collective reading culture produces shared frameworks that the group can use as shorthand.

**As critique:** Use the frameworks you've built to analyze creative work you encounter. Seeing the Capslock framework operating in a brand decision, or the Obstacle Is the Way operating in a management situation, is how the lens gets installed permanently rather than temporarily.

### Level 4: Building a Physical Reference Library

A physical library has a different quality of presence than a reading list. "My house is covered in books on every surface." The books you can reach for, that you can open at any page and find a useful idea, that signal to everyone in your space that this is a place where thinking happens — these function differently than a Kindle library or a note-taking system.

Specific practices:
- Keep books in the room where you work. Accessibility determines whether you reach for them in the moment of need.
- Keep multiple copies of your most important books — to give away, as Virgil did with the Free Game philosophy and as the creator describes with The Obstacle Is the Way. "I would keep a stack of this book on my desk for whenever anyone complained."
- Organize by use, not by category. The books you return to most often should be most accessible.
- Mark up books you read seriously. The markings are the beginning of your personal index — the record of what mattered to you when you read it.

### Level 5: Contrarian Reading

"The books that I have been really into recently are all for me about shaping the way I think and approach problems because frankly, I feel that is the last piece of nuance we have left here when ChatGPT and Claude are telling us the answers to the majority of things."

The specific practice: read material that argues for positions you currently disagree with, not to be persuaded but to understand how strong arguments are constructed. "The way with which they argue, the way with which they talk, how they present the alternatives, and the way people's minds work and approach it is just a really fascinating thing."

What this builds: the ability to construct your own strong arguments rather than relying on positions you already hold. The person who has read the best argument against their own position is better equipped to defend it — or to recognize when they should update it. In a creative context, this translates to: the ability to find the compelling case for the brief you disagree with, the campaign direction you find obvious, the aesthetic you find tired. That ability makes you more useful as a strategist and more honest as a critic.

## Common Mistakes

**Reading only within your discipline.** A fashion creative who only reads about fashion builds a narrow mental model library. The most interesting fashion thinking often comes from applying frameworks borrowed from outside fashion — oral history, political theory, fiction, cognitive science.

**Treating reading as a status signal rather than a practice.** Building a visible bookshelf is not the same as reading. The test is the application: can you reach for a mental model from your reading when you encounter a relevant problem?

**Abandoning difficult books rather than adapting your approach.** Dense foundational books like Capslock are not meant to be read linearly. When a book is difficult, try skipping to the relevant section rather than abandoning it.

**Volume over quality.** A reading-a-week pace that produces no memorable frameworks is less valuable than a reading-a-month pace with deep engagement. "I tried to pick a list of things I feel really will help shape your mind for the kind of decisions you have to make in the modern world" — selection quality matters more than consumption volume.

**Skipping fiction.** "We are a very non-fiction culture now." Fiction trains a specific cognitive capacity — world-building from the inside — that non-fiction cannot train. The creative who reads no fiction is missing the best available training for the most important creative skill.

**Reading only books other people in your network recommend.** Your peer group is reading the same books and developing the same mental model library. "Even the lens we present is, while unique comparative to what you might see versus traditional media, just one factor." The contrarian and cross-disciplinary reading that your immediate network doesn't prioritize is exactly what you should seek out.

## What Success Looks Like

You have a ready answer when someone asks what you've been reading, and it is something outside your primary discipline that you have a specific opinion about.

When you encounter a creative or strategic problem, you have multiple frameworks to apply to it — not just the one that's most obvious given your background.

Your work contains visible influences from sources that your peers cannot immediately place — because your reference library extends beyond the standard sources for your field.

You have given away books to people who needed them for specific problems. The act of giving a book away is evidence that you have internalized it well enough to recognize where it applies.

Your reading has changed a specific decision you made in the last year — not generally informed your perspective but specifically changed a choice.
