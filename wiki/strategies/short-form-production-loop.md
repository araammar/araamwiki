# Strategy: The Six-Step Short-Form Production Loop

## Summary

Callaway's short-form content operation runs on a six-step loop: Idea → Research → Scripting → Recording → Editing → Posting. The loop sounds simple — the system behind it is not. What makes it work at scale is role specialization (who owns each step), the brain-cloning model that makes delegation possible at the creative level, and a progression from solo execution to team-batched output as skills and systems mature. The most important insight: ideation is the infinite bottleneck; editing is the finite one. Scale by solving ideation first.

---

## The Six Steps in Sequence

### Step 1 — Idea
Source and qualify content ideas from relevant inputs: other creators, Twitter/X, Reddit, YouTube, newsletters, and any other niche-specific channels. Idea sourcing is theoretically bottomless — there is no "done." This is why it is the primary leverage point for delegation and the hardest step to automate.

In Callaway's operation, this step is owned by the **Content Operator** (see below), who pitches a shortlist of developed ideas on a weekly or twice-weekly call. Callaway filters — rejecting weak ideas quickly, approving strong ones — and ends with an actionable list.

### Step 2 — Research
Once an idea is approved, it is developed: find visuals (from company sites, Twitter, YouTube, or AI tools), establish the angle, identify why the idea works and what would make it compelling, and organize all supporting assets in a structured document (typically a Google Doc with one tab per idea). Research is ideally done by the Content Operator so the creator can pull a ready-to-script idea from a list without hunting for materials.

### Step 3 — Scripting
Write the script. In Callaway's operation, this is the step he does personally — roughly 20 minutes per script when he pulls a developed idea from the operator's list. He describes this as currently the hardest step to delegate fully: "The hard part is that first part." Sandcastles (his scripting software) can assist beginners through intermediates, but for advanced creators, no AI scripting tool yet matches the output of a fully developed creator brain.

Script quality is the core determinant of content quality. Everything else is execution.

### Step 4 — Recording
Record the video. Callaway does this personally. AI avatars are available but not yet worth the trade-off: "You could use an AI avatar, but it doesn't save that much time." The on-camera credibility and parasocial trust signal from the creator's real presence is not yet replicable by synthetic alternatives.

### Step 5 — Editing
Hand footage to an editor. The editor applies the established style — visual format, pacing, text overlays, audio, B-roll — and returns a finished video. Editing is the step with the clearest delegation path because it's the most explainable: you give the editor the footage, a style guide, and established templates, and they execute.

Callaway runs two full-time editors (based in the Philippines, hired through an agency). Each produces approximately one video per day, 1.5 days for complex visual work. On downtime days with no footage, editors are directed to build templates and systems so they can go faster — systematizing their own workflow during gaps in production.

Editors are given virtual credit cards to sign up for any AI visual generation tools they identify as useful. They self-manage their own tooling to increase speed autonomously.

### Step 6 — Posting
Distribute the finished video. This step is not detailed extensively in Callaway's framework, but it includes platform selection, caption/description copy, relevant hashtags (secondary signal), and timing. For short-form, consistency of posting frequency matters more than optimal timing.

## The Content Operator / Ideas Guy Role

The content operator is the highest-leverage hire for a creator who wants to scale volume without losing quality. The role is upstream of editing — ideas and research, not production.

**What the content operator does:**
- Monitors "idea pipes" (named input channels: relevant creators, Twitter, Reddit, YouTube, newsletters, niche-specific sources)
- Curates a shortlist of ideas with potential
- Develops each shortlisted idea: finds relevant visuals, identifies the angle, explains why it's interesting and what you could do with it
- Pitches the shortlist to the creator in a structured format: (1) what the idea is, (2) why it's timely, (3) why it's good, (4) what you could do with it, (5) the cons or reasons not to make it, (6) their recommendation
- Manages the Google Doc database of developed, ready-to-script ideas

**What the content operator does not do (in Callaway's model initially):** Write final scripts. The dream state is an operator who handles both idea and script and hands the creator a finished script to record. Callaway describes this as where he is trying to get: "That's the dream."

**The pitch call format:** Once or twice a week, the operator presents the developed idea list like a Hollywood showrunner pitching to an executive. The creator's role is filter: reject the weak ones fast, approve the winners, end with an actionable short list.

**Why this role is hard to fill:** Good content operators need creativity (to identify and develop ideas) AND process orientation (to manage pipes, develop systematically, and document consistently). This combination is rare. Hiring paths: (1) offshore/low-cost but intensive training required; (2) find someone who is already doing this kind of creative-process work and wants to do it within a focused operation.

## The Brain Cloning Model

Callaway's framing for delegation: "As an entrepreneur, you're just in the cloning business. I'm trying to clone myself as much as possible into people and then they become me and they can do the things to a level that's close to me."

The cloning model acknowledges that delegation of creative judgment is fundamentally different from delegation of execution tasks. You cannot hand someone a job description and expect them to make good creative calls. You have to transfer judgment — which takes time, intentionality, and a structured method.

**The cloning process stages:**
1. Raw hire — no context, no calibration
2. Aim them — point them at the right inputs and outputs
3. Direction — get them moving toward the right quality standard
4. Nuance — work through edge cases, taste refinement
5. Execution — have them do the work independently
6. Review and correct — run rapid feedback loops until calibration is locked

**Timeline:** 3–4 months of intensive transfer to fully calibrate a content operator. The end state: the operator is "legit as good at finding ideas as I am, and has the systematization to do it."

## The 180-Page Brain Document

The brain document is the physical artifact of the cloning process: a comprehensive Google Doc that encodes Callaway's content judgment so it can be transferred to an operator.

**What it contains:**
- What makes a good idea in his niche
- How to look for ideas across idea pipes
- How to filter good vs. great ideas
- How to develop ideas (what goes in each tab)
- Taste examples: what Callaway approved, what he rejected, and why
- Frameworks, style notes, vocabulary

**How it was created:** Not written from scratch. Captured during live calls and Loom recordings in real time, as Callaway was reviewing work with the operator. Record your reasoning in the moment rather than trying to reconstruct it in writing afterward. "180-page Google Doc" — this is not excess, it is what it actually takes to encode creative judgment at this level of nuance.

**How new hires use it:** A new content operator watches all of Callaway's published curriculum content first (building shared vocabulary and framework understanding), then consumes the brain document. They emerge using Callaway's terminology by default — before any live collaboration begins.

**The remaining 10%:** The brain document gets a new hire to ~90% calibration. The final 10% — taste — can only be transmitted through rapid, high-volume yes/no feedback loops. "We have to go through hundreds of ideas and I have to yes/no/yes/no so he can learn what I actually think. But that's how you clone yourself."

## Sandcastles Scripting Software

Sandcastles is scripting software Callaway built to automate or assist the scripting step of the production loop.

Current assessment from Callaway: "Good, but not as good as me off the dome yet."

**Best use case:** Level 1–6 creators (beginner to intermediate). Can significantly help reduce the time and difficulty of scripting by providing structure and prompts built around proven frameworks.

**Limitation:** For level 9–10 creators, no AI scripting platform — including Sandcastles — can match the output of a fully developed creator brain. Scripting at the highest level requires judgment, taste, and niche-specific voice that current AI cannot replicate.

**Position in the workflow:** Sandcastles sits at Step 3 (Scripting). For operators who don't yet have a content operator trained up, Sandcastles can also assist with Step 2 (Research) by providing proven idea templates and formats.

## The Batching Debate: Beginners vs. Advanced

**Beginners: Do NOT batch.** The first 300 reps should be one video at a time. Every video is a learning rep. Batching compresses learning by producing multiple videos before getting feedback from the first one. If your first video in a batch has a structural problem and you made five more with the same structure, you've compounded a mistake rather than catching it.

"For the first 300 reps, I was going one at a time because I wanted to get learning up."

**Intermediate (current Callaway practice):** 2–3 videos per batch. Fundamentals are established enough that batching produces consistent-quality output, and the feedback loop is robust enough to catch errors at a higher level.

**Advanced (target state):** 5–10 videos per batch. Full team system operational — content operator delivering developed ideas in bulk, editors running parallel, posting cadence systematized. The creator records multiple videos in a single session and hands them all off.

The evolution is sequential: solo one-at-a-time → light batching with strong fundamentals → systematic team batching at scale. Jumping ahead of your actual skill and system readiness collapses quality and learning simultaneously.

## Editor Management System

**Team composition:** Two dedicated full-time editors. Having two (not one) editors means production is never blocked by one person's workload. The editors work in parallel.

**Capacity:** Each editor produces approximately 1 video per day (1.5 days for complex visual work requiring extensive AI-generated assets or motion graphics).

**Downtime protocol:** When there is no footage to edit, editors are directed to spend the day building templates and systems so they can both go faster. "I want you to spend the whole day building templates and systems for the other one so that you guys can go faster." Downtime is not rest — it is systematization investment.

**Tooling:** Editors have virtual credit cards to sign up for any AI tool they identify as useful for generating visuals faster. This creates autonomous tool exploration within the editing function — the editor identifies and adopts the best tools without requiring the creator's involvement.

**Editor hiring path:** Agency-sourced, Philippines-based. Agency model reduces management overhead compared to independent freelancers.

## The Iteration Evolution from Solo to Team Batching

The production system is not built all at once. Callaway's own progression:

- **Early phase (first ~300 reps):** Solo, one video at a time, maximizing iteration learnings per rep. No team. No batch.
- **Middle phase (current):** Content operator handling ideas/research, two editors handling post-production, 2–3 videos batched per session. Callaway handles scripting and recording only.
- **Target phase:** Eventually 5–10 videos per batch with the operator potentially handling scripting handoffs as well, enabling Callaway to record an entire week's worth of content in a single afternoon.

The "just-in-time" confession: Callaway self-describes as currently operating just-in-time (no planning more than a day or two ahead). He acknowledges this as a weakness — the mature system should have a pipeline of developed ideas ready to script and record at any time, not assembled day-of.

## Numbered Series Binge Architecture

A specific short-form format that creates binge-watch pull: numbered video series. "Part 1 of X," "Episode 3 of the frameworks series," etc.

The mechanism: once a viewer enjoys Part 1, they are psychologically compelled to watch Part 2. The series structure creates forward expectation — each video opens a loop that pulls the viewer to the next. This is a structural binge trigger that is independent of individual video quality.

Applies to both short-form and long-form. In short-form, series structure is particularly powerful because the viewer is already in a high-scroll environment. Stopping to find Part 2 requires a search or profile visit — and both create engagement signals that help the algorithm.

The production implication: when you identify a topic rich enough for multiple videos, structure it explicitly as a series from the start. Number the videos, keep the naming consistent, and optimize each subsequent video's hook for "continuing from where we left off."

## When to Use

- **Solo creators, 0–300 reps:** Run the loop yourself, all six steps. Prioritize learning over speed. Do not batch. Do not delegate yet.

- **Creators with some revenue and inconsistent output:** The first hire should be a content operator, not an editor. Ideation is the infinite bottleneck. Editing is finite and can wait. Removing ideation from your plate unlocks consistency.

- **Scaling with a team:** Use the brain document model to transfer creative judgment. Build the document via recorded calls and Looms in real time. Run yes/no feedback loops to calibrate taste.

- **Established teams seeking higher throughput:** Implement batch recording sessions (2–3 then 5–10), develop the operator toward scripting ownership, and systematize editor downtime toward template building.

---

## What to Steal

The most underused insight: ideation is the infinite bottleneck, not editing. Most creators hire editors first because editing is the most obvious outsourcing opportunity. But editing is time-bounded and finite. You can solve it in a week. Ideas take infinite time if you let them. The first outsourcing priority should be the ideas function — even at a basic level of having someone compile and organize potential ideas for your review.

The brain document method is the second steal: create your creative knowledge base through recorded live calls and Looms, not written documentation. Record yourself giving feedback. Record yourself evaluating ideas. Let the recordings accumulate into a transferable knowledge base. You already know what you know — you just need to capture it in a format others can learn from.
