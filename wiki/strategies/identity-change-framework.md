# Strategy: Identity Change Framework

## What This Is For

This framework is for creatives who want to deliberately change who they are — not just their habits or skills, but their fundamental self-presentation, their internal operating system, and how other people experience them. It applies when:

- You have a clear picture of the kind of person you want to be but feel stuck as the person you currently are
- You keep reverting to old behaviors despite good intentions
- The people around you hold a model of you that feels like it's holding you back
- You want to transform how you show up in professional scenarios, relationships, and creative work

The host's own case study: he was a party-loving, laid-back social center through his 20s. He made a deliberate decision to become "intense, focused, driven" — someone who isn't a great casual hang unless you're building something. It took years of deliberate effort. The result is a fundamentally different person, not a performance layered over the old one.

## The Framework

### Stage 0: Determine If This Is Your Year

Before beginning, audit which of the five year-types applies to you:

1. **Money Year** — your finances are chaotic and you lack the foundation to change much else
2. **Systems Year** — you are too disorganized to sustain any deliberate change
3. **Rebrand/Identity Year** — you know what you want to become and are ready to pursue it for a full year
4. **Master Year** — you want to add a specific skill to your identity
5. **Obsession Year** — you already have momentum and want to go all-in on a goal

The identity change framework specifically belongs to the Rebrand Year. If you haven't had a Systems Year, doing the Rebrand Year will be harder — chaos undermines sustained behavioral change. Pick one, not all five.

### Stage 1: Build the Archetype

Write out who you want to become in behavioral, not aspirational terms. The archetype is not a mood board or a vague aspiration. It is a specific operational description:

- How does this person dress in each context?
- What do they say when they're talking to someone they find attractive?
- What do they say when they're negotiating a deal?
- What is their tone and posture in a scenario at work?
- What do they read? What do they consume?
- How do they spend their free time?
- What do they not do that you currently do?

Write these out as actual actions, not adjectives. "This person doesn't explain themselves when criticized" is more useful than "this person is confident."

### Stage 2: Plan Daily as the New Identity

Every evening, before the next day, or every morning: run through the upcoming day and ask "what would this person do in the scenarios I'm going to see?"

Apply it specifically:
- In this meeting tomorrow at work: what would they say, how would they sit, what would they not say?
- At this hang with these friends: how would they engage differently than I currently do?
- With this free time: what would they choose?

Plan the outfit. Plan the conversation. **Walk through that conversation in your head. Have the argument in the shower in the new tone of voice.** The pre-rehearsal is not optional — it is where the new identity is built before you need to perform it under pressure.

### Stage 3: Deploy the Pause

The pause is the most powerful single micro-technique in identity change. It works because identity-driven behavior is automatic and fast. Pausing interrupts the automatic response and creates a beat — long enough to choose the identity-aligned response instead.

Practice: "If someone asks you a question, if you have a scenario come up, taking a moment to think. Other people won't even notice. You take a breath. You think. If you consider what you're going to say before you say it, it's almost respectable."

The inner process: "Am I immediately reacting, or what would I actually want to say? Have that inner narrative, have that moment. Adjust and then act."

This is a daily, in-the-moment practice. Every time you successfully deploy the pause, you have made one repetition toward the new identity.

### Stage 4: Sustain for a Full Year, No Exceptions

The timeline is non-negotiable. "People think they can just change their outfit and they're going to be a different person next month. That's not the case."

One year of:
- Nightly or morning archetype planning
- Consistent deployment of the pause
- Acting as the new identity in scenarios where the old identity would have taken over
- Not making exceptions because it's inconvenient, because people are confused, or because it feels like a performance

"In a year, you can do it. In a month, it is extremely unlikely."

### Stage 5: Hold the Course Through Social Friction

The people around you will react. Some will be confused. Some will test the old identity to see if it's still accessible. "People will be weird about it for whatever amount of time." This is expected and does not require explanation or justification. "You have to pursue what you want and what you are going to do."

Do not explain the change to people who didn't ask. Do not apologize for becoming someone different. Let the new behavior speak.

### Stage 6: The Obsession Year (Advanced)

If the Rebrand Year isn't enough — if you want to completely restructure your life around a goal — the Obsession Year is the maximum-intensity version. It requires:

- **Full prioritization of the goal over everything else.** "Every single action you take is supporting the goal. If it's not, don't do it."
- **Explicit negotiation with people close to you.** Tell them what this year means, what it will look like, what you're trading for it. The host had this conversation with his wife — the result was she joined the project rather than resenting it.
- **Acceptance of the costs.** "It's going to be a long year, it's going to be a hard year, it's probably going to be bad for your health." These are not bugs — they are the nature of the trade.
- **A defined end date.** The obsession year ends. At the end, you evaluate: continue, intensify, or step back. "Give yourself that year cuz it's not forever."

## The Details

**The five year-types are mutually exclusive.** Trying to do a money year and a rebrand year simultaneously splits the focus that makes either work. "You can't do it all. You have to pick one thing. This is where everyone goes wrong."

**Archetype specificity matters.** "How do they dress? What do they say in different scenarios? When they're talking to someone of the opposite sex? When they're negotiating a deal?" The more specific, the more actionable the daily planning becomes.

**Resistance to self-promotion is an identity issue, not a values issue.** The host's realization that he was "proud of the lack of promotion" was an identity realization — he had constructed a self-image around restraint that was actively preventing his work from reaching people. Changing this required explicitly building a new identity that included celebration and visibility.

**The pause works because other people don't notice it.** This is not a polite fiction — it is literally true. A 3-second pause before answering is invisible to the person asking. It feels enormous internally and is barely perceptible externally.

## Common Mistakes

**Picking too many years simultaneously.** If you are trying to radically change your finances, master a new skill, and rebrand yourself in the same calendar year, none of the three will happen.

**Setting a monthly timeline.** One month of behavioral change produces one month of behavioral change. It does not produce identity change. The timescale for the identity model to update is measured in many months, not weeks.

**Explaining the change to everyone.** Announcing a rebrand invites scrutiny and creates social accountability that can backfire. Do the work. Let the results speak.

**Treating social friction as a signal to revert.** People around you will resist the new identity because it disrupts their model of you. This friction is progress, not a problem.

**Making exceptions.** "You can't make an exception. You make an exception, you don't lock in on it." The value of any behavioral commitment is that it is unconditional. An unconditional commitment of 52 weeks turns a chaotic person into an action person. The same commitment with exceptions does not.

## What Success Looks Like

- The new behaviors feel natural rather than performed — you are not acting as the new identity, you are the new identity
- Other people who meet you now have a fundamentally different experience than people who knew you before
- The scenarios that used to trigger old identity-driven reactions now produce new responses automatically, without the pause
- You are making decisions from the new identity rather than negotiating with the old one
- "In a year, you can do it" — meaning: by the end of the year, the archetype you wrote at the start is a description of who you already are
